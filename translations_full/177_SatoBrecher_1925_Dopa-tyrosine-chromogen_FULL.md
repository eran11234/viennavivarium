## I. Introduction.

*Bloch* (1917, 1922) holds the view that melanin formation in the vertebrates does not arise through the action of a tyrosinase, as has been demonstrated for invertebrates by *v. Fürth* (1902), *Przibram* (communicated by *v. Fürth* 1903), *Dewitz* (1904), *Brecher* (1917) and others, but comes about through a ferment specific for dopa (dioxyphenylalanine), a dopa-oxidase. *Bloch* arrived at this view because, on placing frozen sections of the skin in tyrosine, no blackening whatever arose in the tissue, whereas on placing the sections in dopa, those sites blackened where pigment arises in vivo. From this *Bloch* concluded the presence of a specific dopa-oxidase at the sites of pigment formation. That the chromogen is dopa, *Bloch* had not demonstrated.

*Przibram*, with the collaboration of *Dembowski* and *Brecher* (1921), thereupon showed that dopa also blackens spontaneously very rapidly, whereas tyrosine does so only after a very long time; further, that dopa blackens still more rapidly on slight addition of alkali, and most rapidly on addition of aqueous tyrosinase solution; it is therefore quite unnecessary to assume a specific dopa-oxidase.

Experiments by *Onslow* (1915), which owing to the war came to our knowledge only late, have shown that in vertebrates too the tyrosinase is to be regarded as the melanin-forming ferment; extracts of black or brown skin areas of quite young rabbits blackened tyrosine, though only on addition of small quantities of H₂O₂; extracts of albinotic animals gave no blackening of the tyrosine. Extracts of skins or skin areas of dominant white gave no blackening of the tyrosine; rather, they even inhibited the reaction as soon as H₂O₂ was added to tyrosine + extract of coloured skin areas. *Onslow* explains this by the assumption of an inhibiting factor in the skins of dominant white.

Thus a tyrosinase does in fact seem to have been demonstrated in vertebrates also. The demonstration succeeds, however, only with the addition of H₂O₂. Following the theory of *Bach* and *Chodat*, that tyrosinase consists of an oxidase and a peroxide, *Onslow* assumes that the peroxide is lost during the working-up of the skin extract, and he replaces it in the experiment by the addition of H₂O₂.

Nevertheless it would perhaps be theoretically conceivable that tyrosine, on addition of H₂O₂, is converted into dopa, and that in the vertebrate skins it is not tyrosine but dopa that constitutes the chromogen for melanin formation.

Dopa as chromogen was established by *Przibram* (1922) in the cocoons of certain insects.

In the enzymatic melanin formation by tyrosinase in the invertebrates, tyrosine appears to have been demonstrated as the chromogen (*v. Fürth* in *Przibram*, *Dembowski* and *Brecher* 1921, and *Schulz* 1901, cited after *Schulz* 1923).

It was now very natural, in view of the question of pigment formation in vertebrates, to examine skin extracts of vertebrates for the presence of one of these two chromogens, dopa or tyrosine, which come into consideration in the first place for melanin formation.

## II. Experiments for the Demonstration of Dopa in the Skin Extracts of Vertebrates.

### a) Method.

The investigations were carried out on the skin and skin covering (scales, feathers, hair) of fishes (crucian carp, roach), birds (fowl) and mammals (rabbit, rat, guinea-pig, pig, dog, cat, horse and monkey), pigmented and unpigmented areas, quite young as well as adult animals, being drawn upon for the investigation, as appears indicated in the accompanying table.

For the preparation of the skin extract the animals were killed and the skin was freed as well as possible from the subcutaneous fatty tissue and, in most cases, from the skin covering (scales, feathers, hair), then very finely minced with scissors or, in some experiments, ground in a mortar. This finely minced mass was now put into a test tube and boiling distilled water was poured over it, so that the water just covered the minced skin. After an extraction period of ½–1 hour the aqueous extract was distributed among small test tubes, and with these the dopa reactions described further below were now carried out.

The scales or hairs were likewise cut up very small and treated in the test tube in exactly the same way as the pieces of skin.

*Dopa reactions*: Dopa, as a dioxyphenyl(alanine), gives on addition of dilute ferric chloride solution a green colouration, which on addition of saturated sodium carbonate changes to purple-red.

Addition of ammoniacal silver nitrate to dopa results in a blackening through the deposition of silver. This latter reaction, however, is not characteristic of dopa alone. Other chromogens and pigments also have this property.

In the present experiments one drop of a strongly diluted ferric chloride solution was added to 2 cm³ of extract. This quantity of extract treated with ferric chloride was now divided between two small test tubes, and to one of them a further drop of concentrated sodium carbonate solution was added.

To another sample of the original extract a 2% ammoniacal silver nitrate solution was added.

### c) Control Experiments to Establish the Limit of Sensitivity of these Dopa Reactions.

For this purpose 0.0018 g of dopa was dissolved in 4 cm³ of distilled water, that is, a 0.45‰ solution was prepared. Starting from this stock solution, a 4-fold, 16-fold, 64-fold etc. dilution was prepared.

With these various dilutions of dopa solution the dopa reactions with ferric chloride, or ferric chloride + sodium carbonate, and with silver nitrate were now carried out at a room temperature of 17 °C.

The ferric chloride and the ferric chloride + sodium carbonate reactions turned out positive, and indeed immediately after addition of the reagent, with the stock solution and with the four-fold diluted solution.

With the following dilutions the characteristic colourations had not appeared even after longer standing.

The ferric chloride and ferric chloride + sodium carbonate reaction thus indicates the presence of dopa down to a concentration which lies between 0.1‰ and 0.02‰.

The reaction with silver nitrate turned out immediately strongly positive with the dopa stock solution and with the 4- and 16-fold diluted solutions, and distinctly positive in the course of 20 minutes down to dilution No. 6, that is, down to a 1024-fold dilution (0.00045‰ dopa concentration).

Since in the examination for dopa the silver nitrate reaction alone gives no information, one can say, from the outcome of these control experiments, that on the basis of the ferric chloride reaction dopa can be demonstrated down to a concentration, by dilution, of 0.025‰.

## III. Investigations for the Demonstration of Free Tyrosine in Extracts.

*v. Fürth* (communicated in *Przibram*, with the collaboration of *Dembowski* and *Brecher* 1921) had demonstrated free tyrosine in the pupal blood of *Sphinx ligustri*. The method here was the following: the blood was dropped into boiling distilled water. After filtering off the coagulum, the protein still present in the filtrate was precipitated, after acidification of the filtrate with 10% HCl, by the addition of 10% phosphotungstic acid, and filtered. The filtrate no longer gave any biuret reaction, and was thus completely free of protein. The filtrate was now treated with baryta water to remove the excess of phosphotungstic acid, and filtered. To remove the excess of baryta, CO₂ was passed through the filtrate, which was then filtered again. On cautious addition of Millon's reagent and warming, the filtrate gave the brick-red colouration characteristic of tyrosine. On the other hand, a sample of the filtrate showed blackening on addition of tyrosinase. Free tyrosine was thus hereby demonstrated in the pupal blood as chromogen.

*Schulz* (1901, cited after *Schulz* in Winterstein's Handbuch 1923) observed in the blood of various caterpillar species, on evaporation, crystals which in their external form resembled tyrosine. With these crystals, however, he could obtain a positive Millon's or Piria's reaction neither microscopically nor macrochemically.

In our experiments for the demonstration of free tyrosine we confined ourselves to investigations of the fish skin and, as a control, of the pupal blood of two lepidopteran species.

*Preparation of the extract.* In order to prevent a conversion of any free tyrosine present in the fish skin into melanin by the tyrosinase, the tyrosinase was rendered inactive by rapidly descaling the fishes and throwing them for ½–1 minute into boiling water. The skin was then stripped off and extracted in warm water for one hour. This extract was boiled in order to bring the protein to coagulation.

The extract filtered off from the coagulum was further treated according to the method used by *v. Fürth*. In some experiments we removed the excess of baryta not by passing CO₂ through, as *v. Fürth* had done, but by the addition of 10% sulphuric acid.

The filtrate thus obtained no longer gave any biuret reaction, and was thus completely freed from protein.

A positive outcome of Millon's reaction can now indicate the presence of free tyrosine.

### Experimental Results.

*Crucian carp.* Millon's test turned out negative on the extract obtained by the above method from four crucian carp.

*Roach.* An extract prepared from five animals by the method described above was evaporated to dryness in the drying oven at 55 °C. The residue showed no Millon's reaction.

*Pupae of Sphinx ligustri.* The blood of nine pupae was dropped into 10 cm³ of boiling distilled water and further treated in the manner described earlier. The biuret reaction turned out weakly positive, Millon's reaction negative.

*Pupae of Phalera bucephala* (Mondfleck [buff-tip]). 15 pupae were cut in two and boiled in 40 cm³ of distilled water, and further treated as above. Biuret reaction almost negative, Millon's reaction distinctly positive. This extract blackened on addition of tyrosinase.

An attempt to obtain the tyrosine in crystals, so that the demonstration of tyrosine might succeed in a more elegant manner, remained without success with the method we used: concentration of the extract in the drying oven down to one third of its volume, addition of absolute alcohol (6 parts alcohol to 1 part extract).

The now positive, now negative result of the investigations for the demonstration of tyrosine in pupal blood, and the negative result in the investigations of the fish skin, suggest the suspicion that, with the procedure for removing the protein, any tyrosine present in the extract is in part lost, probably being carried down with the protein, so that at the end it can no longer be demonstrated in the filtrate obtained by this procedure.

To test this idea, we prepared solutions of crystallised hen's-egg albumin and of commercial tyrosine, in various concentrations and mixing ratios, and subjected such mixtures of protein and free tyrosine to the same treatment for the removal of the protein as the fish-skin and pupal-blood extracts.

The solutions used for these experiments were prepared in the following manner:

As can be seen from this table, Millon's reaction is distinctly weakened after the procedure for the removal of the protein, and at weaker concentration the tyrosine is no longer demonstrable at all after the treatment. Whereas tyrosine alone, before the treatment, still gives a positive Millon's reaction down to an 80-fold dilution, in a mixture of protein and tyrosine, after the treatment for the removal of the protein, the tyrosine which was present in the initial mixture in the ratio of 1 to 4 is no longer demonstrable with Millon's reaction.

It is thus natural to assume that in the precipitation of the protein the tyrosine is carried down with it.

From this it follows that in the fish-skin extracts the quantity of tyrosine, probably small in comparison with the quantity of protein, is so reduced by the treatment for the removal of the protein that it is no longer demonstrable with Millon's reaction.

Tyrosine, which after all occurs in the animal body as a breakdown product of protein, could thus yield the chromogen even where its demonstration no longer succeeds after removal of the protein.

It remains reserved for later experiments to try another way to the positive demonstration of free tyrosine in skin extracts.

## IV. Summary.

Whereas in invertebrate animals tyrosine appears to have been demonstrated as the chromogen in the enzymatic melanin formation by tyrosinase (*v. Fürth*, communicated in *Przibram*, with the collaboration of *Dembowski* and *Brecher*: Action of tyrosinase on dopa, 1921), the question of the chromogen in the vertebrates is still open, although after *Onslow's* (1915) experiments the occurrence of a tyrosinase as the melanin-forming ferment in the skins of mammals too can probably no longer be doubted. Besides tyrosine, dopa also occurs in the invertebrates as a melanin-former, but has so far been demonstrated only in the cocoons of certain insects (*Przibram* 1922, *Sciacchitano* 1923). Since *Bloch* (1917) regards precisely dopa as the probable basis for pigment formation in the vertebrates, it was of interest to search for the occurrence of this substance in the pigment-forming tissues. The examination of skin and skin covering in fishes, birds and mammals turned out completely negative; neither the characteristic green colouration on addition of ferric chloride nor the change to purple-red on adding sodium carbonate could be observed in the extracts, such as dopa solutions and aqueous extracts of the cocoons mentioned always show. Dopa is thus certainly not the usual chromogen of the vertebrate skin and its covering.

By means of Millon's reaction tyrosine can be demonstrated in all the extracts named, but it remains undecided thereby whether free tyrosine is really indicated, or whether it merely becomes recognisable through the reaction in the protein present. When boiled extracts of various lepidopteran pupae and fish skins were freed from protein according to the procedure of *v. Fürth* (phosphotungstic acid, baryta water, carbonic acid or 10% sulphuric acid), Millon's reaction turned out now negative, now positive. The suspicion that this behaviour is to be ascribed to a substantial destruction of the tyrosine by the drastic procedures for protein removal was confirmed in mixtures of hen's-egg albumin and tyrosine, which after the same treatment showed a very substantial weakening of Millon's reaction, up to its disappearance.

Tyrosine, which is present in the skins and skin coverings, as indeed in every protein, could thus yield the chromogen there too, where its demonstration no longer succeeds after removal of the protein.

*Translator's note.* This short biochemical note is No. X of Przibram's "Ursachen tierischer Farbkleidung" (Causes of Animal Colouration) series and No. 107 of the Institute's *Mitteilungen*; it tests Bruno Bloch's claim that vertebrate melanin is made from dopa by a specific "dopa-oxidase" by looking for dopa (ferric chloride / sodium carbonate colour test) and for free tyrosine (Millon's reaction) directly in skin, scale, feather and hair extracts, and is the companion of Brecher and Winkler's paper XI on frozen sections in the same volume. Term choices: *Ferment* = ferment (the period word for enzyme); *Eisenchlorid* = ferric chloride; *Dioxyphenylalanin* = dioxyphenylalanine (3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine, L-dopa); *Eiweiß* = protein, or albumin where the hen's-egg albumin solution is meant; *Hautbekleidung / Hautbedeckung* = skin covering; the original's ⁰/₀₀ is rendered ‰ (per mille), and "ccm" as cm³. Read today, the negative dopa result and the authors' caution about tyrosine are both sound: dopa is now known to be a short-lived intermediate that tyrosinase itself forms from tyrosine inside the melanocyte, so it is not stored as a chromogen, and Bloch's dopa-oxidase and the tyrosinase of the invertebrate workers are the same enzyme.
