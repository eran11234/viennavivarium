## I. Descriptive Part (by O. Grosser).

### 1. An accessory fin in the head region.

In a half-grown, 56 cm long male specimen, otherwise normally formed, the following abnormality is found: between the spiracles, in the dorsal midline on the *head*, there stands an unpaired, transversely placed, flat fin 4 cm in length and 18 mm in breadth, with a rather irregular outline; it sits on the skull by a narrowed stalk. It is inclined fairly strongly backwards, yet without actually lying against the skull (cf. Plate IV, Fig. 2). Just in front of its root the external openings of the ductus endolymphatici become visible (cf. also Plate IV, Fig. 3).

Dissection shows that the fin is attached to the posterior margin of the parietal fossa by means of a kind of joint; the joint cleft, however, is not a single one, but is subdivided by strands of connective tissue. Moreover, a fold of skin pushes forward from behind so far beneath the stalk of the fin that the joint-like connection remains restricted to the rostral part of the basal surface of the stalk. In accordance with this articular connection, the parietal fossa, when dissected free, shows a slight flattening of its normally steep posterior margin, as well as a very moderate widening, with an increase in the distance between the openings of the cartilaginous canals for the ductus endolymphatici; but these changes are so slight that they are noticeable at all only on direct comparison with a normal specimen (Plate IV, Fig. 4).

In other respects the skull is perfectly normally formed, without special nerve or vessel canals for the fin. The beginning of the vertebral column, too, shows nothing special — at most the direct attachment of the first intercalary to the dorsal margin of the foramen occipitale might be mentioned (Plate IV, Fig. 4). Likewise the arrangement and extent of the dorsal musculature on the skull are entirely in accordance with the norm. The fin itself possesses a right and a left margin, an upper and a lower surface. The right margin bears a rather sharply projecting angle; the left margin is rolled in (Fig. 3); after this inrolling has been undone, a similar angle appears on the left (Text-fig. 1). The distal margin is divided by a notch into two lobes. One might think that the fin is composed of two symmetrical halves, with weaker development of the left half — an impression that the behaviour of the fin skeleton does not contradict either (see the text-figure).

**Text-fig. 1.** [The original gives no legend beyond »Fig. 1.«; the figure shows the skeleton of the accessory head fin, seen from above after removal of the upper fin skin, as described in the following paragraph.]  *(figure not reproduced)*

After removal of the upper fin skin this skeleton, consisting partly of cartilage and partly of horny rays, becomes visible. The cartilaginous skeleton consists of a basal piece on which four rays sit. Of these the strongest lies almost in the middle, while one only slightly weaker forms the right margin. To the left of these two rays lie two further rays, the weakest of all being in the left margin of the fin. The first ray is completely separated from the basal piece and divided into two pieces; the second is fused with the basal piece at its proximal end, but transversely jointed near its middle; the third ray is indeed segmented off from the basal piece, but is fused laterally with the fourth, which passes continuously into the basal piece. The ends of the marginal rays lie in the projecting angles of the lateral margins of the fin. The basal piece bears at its proximal end the cartilaginous articular surface, and on its anterior surface a small tubercle besides. From the distal margins of the rays numerous horny filaments, essentially parallel to one another, run to the free distal margin of the fin.

We find that the degree of differentiation of the rays decreases from the right towards the left margin. In particular, however, the two left rays have lagged behind the two right ones. Taking this circumstance into account, we may perhaps speak, in the case of the skeleton too, of a bilaterally symmetrical Anlage with retardation of the left half. The notch in the distal margin of the fin would assign one pair of rays to each half.

To the question of the innervation and musculature of this fin particular attention was paid from the outset during the dissection, since it seemed that information about the nature of the structure might be obtained from it. Here, however, expectations were thoroughly disappointed. After the skin had been cut through, the fin could be detached from its base quite easily, together with a small flap of connective tissue lying subcutaneously and directed caudalwards, and in the process no macroscopically demonstrable nerves, vessels or muscle bundles came to light at all. To be quite certain, both a strip of the skin of the proximal part of the lower fin surface, with the subcutaneous connective tissue and the perichondrium, and the adhering flap of connective tissue at the base were examined in serial sections. But only quite fine nerve bundles were found, partly of oval cross-section with diameters of at most 63 × 27 µ, partly of circular cross-section up to 45 µ in diameter — thus beyond the limit of macroscopic visibility — nerve bundles such as occur everywhere in the corium; they lay partly in the corium of the fin, partly in the basal flap of connective tissue. Muscle fibres were entirely absent. The fin is thus without muscles and without nerves of its own; the fibres demonstrable in the skin belong to the cutaneous nerves, such as supply the integument in general, and in any case derive from the neighbouring cutaneous nerve territories, without any particular morphological significance being attributable to their presence.

The interpretation of this structure meets with great difficulties. The fin can belong either to the system of the unpaired fins or to the paired fins.

The dorsal fin, if we leave aside *Amphioxus* and a few strongly modified teleosts, does not extend onto the cranium in any recent vertebrate. Here, however, an atavism might be present, since the dorsal fin undoubtedly formerly reached further headwards¹. A fossil fish is also known which bears on the cranium a structure that is regarded by some authors as a head fin, namely *Pleuracanthus gaudryi*. Its systematic position is not yet completely clarified; possibly it belongs to the fossil selachians. As emerges in particular from the investigations of Fritsch and Reis, it possessed a powerful bony spine, laid back posteriorly, which probably sat immovably on the occipital part of the cranium through the intermediary of a cartilaginous peg. The spine shows no traces of muscle attachments. Reis in particular, it is true, pronounces very decidedly against this structure belonging to the unpaired fins, a standpoint whose justification the most recent investigator of this question, K. Fürbringer, does not acknowledge, without, however, being able to decide the question. In no case, however, does this head spine bear any resemblance to the cartilaginous, multi-rayed head fin of our case, articulated with the skull.

> ¹ For the references to the relevant literature we are indebted to a kind communication from H. Braus, Heidelberg.

Altogether, the derivation of the latter from an unpaired fin does not appear possible. Even the head spine of *Pleuracanthus* still lies in the occipital region of the skull, that is, in a region which has arisen from the fusion of vertebral segments with upper (neural) spines (K. Fürbringer); our fin sits in the region of the pars otica of the skull, whose segmentation has long since been lost. Further, one would have to assume for it a rotation through 90° with subsequent folding backwards, since the rays lie not one behind the other but side by side. Finally, the skeleton itself also bears no resemblance whatever to the skeleton of a dorsal fin, in which the antero-posterior difference of the elements is always clearly marked.

There remains, then, the interpretation of the fin as an aberrant paired extremity, and the question of its possible symmetrical Anlage (corresponding to its position in the midline of the body), to be discussed. It is true that the skeleton cannot be described as directly limb-like; but on the other hand the arrangement of the elements does not contradict this interpretation.

The two cases of accessory fins in *Raja batis* recently published by Rennie approach the one described here insofar as there, too, the fins are placed medially or nearly medially. They are found, however, not in the head region but in the trunk region, and their membership of the system of the paired fins is hardly to be doubted; in the case that was examined more closely, part of the fin musculature of one side is even connected with the accessory fin as well. One of the fins recalls phylogenetically lower forms (*Cladoselache*). As an explanation of the abnormality the author thinks of the outgrowth of a displaced germ cell.

### 2. Malformation of the anterior body end.

A very striking malformation of the head was found in a 14 cm long male embryo, which still possessed a yolk sac of 2½ cm longitudinal and 12 mm transverse diameter, with a 2 cm long stalk (Figs. 6 and 7). From the root of the anterior extremity caudalwards the individual is normally formed. On the trunk and on the fins fairly abundant pigmentation has already appeared. The spines of the dorsal fins have not yet broken through the skin.

Immediately behind the eyes the head is very strongly constricted, somewhat more strongly at the lateral parts than in the dorso-ventral direction. As a result the eyes project very strongly from the head. Rostrum, nasal pits and the orbital region are normally shaped.

Inspection from the dorsal side shows that only one spiracle, the left, is present; on the right every trace of it is lacking. Altogether the malformation seems to have affected the right half of the head more strongly than the left, whereby a kinking of the axis of the head in an obtuse angle open to the right comes about; the apex of the angle of kinking lies in the labyrinth region.

More marked than on the dorsal side is the malformation on the ventral side. The mouth opening has been divided into two sections by a broad connecting strand between upper and lower lip; these are displaced towards the lateral surfaces of the head, so that the outer angle of the mouth lies on the left roughly on a horizontal line with the pupil, and on the right even higher, at the level of the upper corneal margin. In front of the mouth cleft, exactly in the middle between the eyes, a stub-like projection (bürzelförmig, like a bird's rump) protrudes from the ventral side.

The mouth openings lead into a pharynx which is very broad at its entrance; a probe introduced into the one opening can very easily be led out at the other opening beneath the bridge which divides the mouth openings. Caudalwards the pharynx narrows rapidly to the normal breadth and bears on each side the normal number of five respiratory gill slits.

The bridge between the mouth openings makes approximately the impression produced in amniote vertebrates by the malformations caused by so-called amniotic bands; a proof that in the latter, too, one must always adduce compelling grounds for the assumption of such a band. To be sure, one might here too perhaps think of an adhesion to the germinal area (Fruchthof) with subsequent detachment. But direct evidence of such a thing, such as a scarred spot in the integument, is not to be found.

Perhaps the most striking finding on this object, however, is the development of gills on the upper jaw. At the left mouth opening eleven sagittally placed lamellae can be demonstrated on the upper jaw, at the (smaller) right one about fifteen. These gill lamellae, as the microscope shows, are beset with small lateral tubercles in which strongly dilated capillary loops lie. The epithelium is quite low, the typical structure of a gill unmistakable.

The mucous membrane of the lower jaw appears macroscopically smooth; microscopically, isolated tooth Anlagen, in any case reduced in number and abnormally young, can be demonstrated.

### 3. Malformation of the posterior body end.

The malformation is found in a relatively large male foetus, in any case already near birth, in which the distance from the tip of the snout to the posterior margin of the anal opening is 12 cm. The total length, measured along the curvature, is 20 cm, but precisely on account of the malformation of the tail this does not correspond to the degree of development of the animal. Comparison with normal specimens, having regard to the first-mentioned figure, gives about 22 cm as the total length of the animal, normal development presupposed (Plate IV, Fig. 5).

The animal still possesses a fairly large yolk sac (4 cm long, 1¾ cm in diameter) with a 2 cm long stalk. The dorsal side and the proximal three-quarters of the pectoral fin show normal pigmentation; the free margin of the pectoral fins and the pelvic fins are still unpigmented. The spine of the first dorsal fin has not yet broken through.

While head and trunk are normally shaped, the malformed section begins roughly at the level of the posterior margin of the anal opening; the changes are thus restricted to the tail. Here, above all, the complete absence of fins is striking; neither the second dorsal nor the caudal fin has come to development. The tail is an almost cylindrical structure, gradually narrowing towards the tip, which somewhat beyond its middle is bent over towards the dorsal side at a fairly acute angle and is rolled in at its tip.

**Text-figs. 2 and 3.** Cross-section through the tail of a normal embryo 19 cm in length, and through that of the malformed specimen. Anterior cut surface. The vertical lines correspond to the sagittal plane of the trunk. Magnification 7½. [Labels in the figure: *Med*, *Ch*, *Ao* — spinal cord, notochord, aorta.]  *(figures not reproduced)*

The dorsal midline, projecting as a ridge, turns spirally to the left just behind the anal region and loses itself on the ventral side. First, however, it forms here the left boundary of a groove whose right boundary is formed by a second, weaker ridge, which in position and course corresponds approximately to a normal ventral midline. Around the end of these ridges there runs, in an arc open orally, a third, weakly marked ridge.

The cross-section of the tail (Text-fig. 2) shows spinal cord, notochord, aorta, and a system of roughly radially placed longitudinal muscle plates. Nevertheless it differs not inconsiderably from the cross-section of a normal tail. Above all it is rotated relative to the trunk, but contrary to the spiral course of the dorsal midline, in such a way that its plane of symmetry runs from upper right to lower left and encloses an angle of about 40° with that of the trunk. Further, the muscles are not, as in the normal animal, on both sides of the plane of symmetry, but distributed evenly around the axial structures; also, in accordance with the rounded shape of the tail, the flattening of the ventral side is lacking.

The pigmentation of the tail piece, too, is irregular. On the right half of its upper surface a proximally situated piece and the end that is turned forwards are pigmented; on the left only the continuation of the midline running down over the lateral surface and the rolled-in tip; otherwise the tail is as unpigmented as the ventral surface of the animal in general.

We are thus dealing here with a shortened, rolled-in, finless, rudimentary tail, twisted relative to the body and incompletely pigmented.

### 4. Two cases of duplicitas anterior.

Although the two cases to be briefly described here offer hardly anything special from the standpoint of teratology, their mention may nevertheless be justified on two grounds. First, because they are probably the first to be described in a viviparous squalid; and secondly, because they are two fairly widely separated developmental stages of almost the identical malformation.

In both cases (Plate IV, Figs. 8–11) we are again dealing with embryos. But the one case corresponds to a fairly advanced stage; the (female) malformation is about 14 cm long, the external body form accordingly almost completely developed; only a yolk sac, still about 4 cm long including the stalk, and the pigmentation, restricted to a stripe along the dorsal side, characterise the foetus. The younger specimen is only about 26 mm long, sits by a thin stalk on the still very large (approximately spherical) yolk sac, is still entirely free of pigment (also in the retina), and possesses external gill filaments which are only a few millimetres long. The triangular mouth is still wide open, the spiracle only slightly displaced dorsalwards.

The cleft formation is (in both specimens) total as far as the attachment of the yolk-sac stalk; there are thus two normally developed heads and branchial baskets. From the yolk-sac stalk onwards the structures of the ventral side are unpaired; there are thus only two pairs of extremities, as well as one unpaired ventral half of the caudal fin. The structures of the dorsal side are doubled throughout; thus two crests corresponding to the dorsal midline, two pairs of dorsal fins and two dorsal caudal-fin halves are developed. The caudal fin accordingly appears three-rayed; the heterocercy finds expression only in the fact that the ventral part of the fin is about half as long as the dorsal ones.

A cross-section between the second dorsal and the caudal fin shows (in the larger specimen) that spinal cord, notochord and aorta are doubled. Just ventral to the aorta the two sagittal planes unite. On the lower (outer) side the musculature is normally developed and the dorsal and ventral sections are differentiated as well; on the upper (inner) side this separation is lacking, and the ventral Anlagen of the two sides pass into one another without a distinct boundary.

## II. Theoretical Part (by H. Przibram).

While the earliest collectors of natural curiosities sought out precisely abnormalities most eagerly, the collecting of such objects later fell into discredit, since they were of no value for the systematic museums and also seldom seemed to offer any phylogenetic interest. Only when »developmental mechanics« had once more directed its attention to the causes of development, and it became possible by experiment to produce many malformations at will, did the value of the abnormalities found in nature, these products of »experiments of nature«, begin to rise again.

Therefore, at the founding of the Biological Experimental Institute in Vienna, it seemed appropriate to attach to this institution, devoted to developmental-mechanical experiment, a museum in which not only the experimental results obtained in the Institute itself, but also preparations of other experimenters and malformations found in nature, could be brought to permanent preservation and appropriate display. A certain basic stock was provided by the older preparations from the experiments of Przibram and Kammerer, and this has been growing rapidly during the working years of the Institute, notably in the botanical field as well. If, however, the purpose of the »developmental-mechanical museum« is to be fully achieved, support from other researchers, by sending in preparations of their experimental results and occasionally obtained monstrosities, is necessary. Wilhelm Roux has, at our request, already promised his cooperation, recognising the idea of such a collection, as have Gustav Tornier and some other developmental mechanicians. I should like to use this opportunity to address a public appeal for the furtherance of our museum, through donations of the kinds of preparations already indicated, to all those who have an interest in our field of research and at the same time wish to secure the permanent preservation of the results of their work.

Among the first donations that fell to our collection were the shark malformations described in the present paper, all of which Prof. K. Cori, as director of the Imperial-Royal Zoological Station at Trieste, had the opportunity to collect. For the kind donation of the material we hereby express our gratitude.

The foregoing descriptive treatment of the shark malformations thus forms, as it were, the beginning of a catalogue of the collection. If I go on to say a few words about the significance of the preparations for problems of developmental mechanics, this is done in order to demonstrate the intimate relations of such teratological findings to the theoretical and experimental science of form-formation.

Deviations from the normal constitution of a species may relate either to the repetition of a character that is otherwise also present, in too great or too small a number or at an improper place — which Bateson has termed »meristic variation« — or to the new appearance of characters which are not ordinarily observed at all on the body of the animal species concerned, Bateson's »substantive variation«.

According to the time of acquisition of the deviating character, it may be designated either as a spontaneous germinal variation by the animal itself, or as an acquired one.

This latter distinction can be made with certainty only where experiments are concerned, but not where, as with our sharks, natural finds are concerned. Then we are dependent on comparison with analogous malformations whose causal connection could be ascertained by experiment.

By far the most frequent acquisitions which, owing to their strong deviation from the norm, force themselves upon the student of variation are to be traced back, directly or indirectly, to regeneration. So too the greatest number of the cases designated by Bateson as »meristic variation«, which this investigator originally sought to interpret as spontaneous »discontinuous variation«, but which he is at present (as he has informed me by word of mouth) inclined to recognise as regenerations.

The regenerates may deviate from the normal shape of the body part concerned in that they have reproduced it incompletely (defect formations), or in that, owing to the presence of several wound sites, they have brought forth multiple formations, or finally in that they have set a part different from the one that previously existed in its place (heteromorphosis).

Besides regeneration, there come into consideration as explanations of acquired characters: inhibition before the first differentiation of the part concerned, splitting before the first development, and fusions. Often it will be a matter of a combination of several of these causes.

1) The accessory fin in the head region recalls other cases in which a limb grows at a place on the body where it neither should normally stand, nor (as in the heteromorphoses) replaces another limb that is missing in its stead. Such cases are rare; the following can be cited: *Carcinus maenas*, shore crab with a right walking leg growing out on the left side of the normally legless sixth abdominal segment (described by Bethe, see Bibliography); *Dilophus tibialis*, a midge, with an antenna growing on the coxal segment of a foreleg (described by Wheeler, see Bibliography). Wheeler calls this kind of repetition of an organ that stands at another place — in contrast to homoeosis (Bateson), in which the substitution of one limb by another takes place — adventive homoeosis (English: »redundant or adventitious«). Among the vertebrates quite analogous cases have hitherto not been known; but I believe that the reason for this lies in the assumption generally made (e.g. also by Bethe in his *Carcinus* paper) that those frequently occurring monstrosities in which a pair of extremities grows out, usually from the neck, are to be regarded as remnants of two embryos fused with one another, of which one has »consumed« the other except for the remaining pair of extremities. I have had the opportunity of seeing such formations alive on a calf and a goat in a show booth. It struck me that between the extremities, which complement one another as mirror images, an intercalated piece of vertebral column appears to be lacking, so that the shoulder-girdle pieces of the two extremities are grown *directly* together. Now this does not agree with the assumption of a »remnant of a second animal«. On the other hand, the symmetry relations are in accordance with »Bateson's law«, which holds for supernumerary formations that stand at the *organ-appropriate* place, notably in the »triplicate formations«. Now since the latter, as emerges especially from the analytical and experimental investigations of Tornier, are to be derived from fractures with subsequent regeneration from both gaping wound surfaces, it is natural to think of a similar origin for the »doubled vertebrate extremities«. Indeed, I know from my own observation a case in which a symmetrical pair of legs sat on the right shoulder blade of a bull in such a way that it fulfilled the conditions for derivation from a fracture of the shoulder girdle (in embryonic life) with subsequent super-regeneration just as exactly as the frog with a similar malformation described by Tornier. For the additional limbs that do not stand at the correct place, a complete splitting-off of a corresponding piece from the embryonic Anlage of the organ concerned will naturally have to be assumed. This piece contains what is necessary for the development of the limb concerned; but it depends on the position of its free surfaces whether it differentiates further only into a single limb or into a symmetrical pair. The possibility of the further development of completely split-off Anlagen transplanted to foreign body sites has, after all, been established by the experiments of Born, Crampton and especially Braus; the frequent origin of multiple formations with secondary symmetry relations is particularly clear from Tornier's latest publication on experimentally produced many-legged toads; in particular, Braus has in his experiments directly observed on several occasions the appearance of accessory mirror images to a *single* implanted extremity; there thus remains only the experimental proof that this takes place precisely in those cases in which the central stump of the extirpated Anlage did not grow on completely, but left a free surface over, from which the mirror-image regenerate grew¹. If we apply this hypothesis, supported by many facts, to the head fin of the shark, we shall have to regard it as a displaced Anlage, originally belonging to the pectoral fins, which in consequence of its position in the midline of the body presented free surfaces of equal standing towards both sides, and therefore developed with secondary symmetry. At what time the displacement took place is difficult to say; but an apportionment effected before the formation of the Anlage of the anterior extremity is hardly probable, because at early stages the embryonic cells of fishes possess a strong regulatory capacity (according to the experiments especially of Morgan and Sommer [sc. Sumner]), so that presumably a different utilisation, instead of self-differentiation into a fin, would have taken place.

> ¹ The absence of the nerve in the »accessory« mirror-image limbs I interpret as the inability of the peripheral nerve to regenerate a new nerve ending centralwards. It would be interesting to examine whether, of the supernumerary mirror-image extremities occurring in monsters that have arisen naturally and through fractures (e.g. the cited cases in *Bos*), one is not nerveless. Our case in the shark, on account of the rudimentary development of the accessory fin, is not usable for the discussion of this question despite the absence of the nerves.

An indication that from the outset an unequal nuclear partition takes place, as Bethe infers from the *Carcinus* he described, I therefore cannot see in the cases of adventive homoeosis, since the displacement of the Anlage may very well have taken place only late, precisely when the cells had already forfeited their totipotency. Indeed, given the high regenerative power of the crustaceans it is not excluded that Bethe's crab suffered the injury, during which the »autotransplantation« of the displaced Anlage took place, actually post-embryonically. This question is accessible to experiment, and so is the further one, whether pieces of Anlage implanted in the midline of the body assume secondary symmetry; hence our hypothesis in any case possesses a certain heuristic value. As regards, further, the path that the split-off piece had to travel to its new position, it must be borne in mind that in the embryo the relative position of the different parts is different from that in the developed animal¹.

> ¹ One may recall, for example, the curled-up position of the embryo in the amniotes and insects. Thus the malformation of *Dilophus tibialis* described by Wheeler may possibly be traced back to a germ displacement of an antennal Anlage onto the leg, if one considers that the head, bent downwards in the egg, draws the origin of the antenna quite close to the coxa of the foreleg. The greater size of the accessory antenna I explain to myself by its position on a limb that receives a much stronger blood stream. Perhaps the greater elongation of the segments, which Wheeler interprets as an atavistic character, is also connected with this.

2) The »malformation of the anterior body end« is to be designated as a true »substitutional« homoeosis, in that in the upper jaw a gill Anlage has developed *in place of* the tooth Anlage; if it were a matter of regeneration, the expression »heteromorphosis« would be in place. The other formations deviating from the norm make a mechanical action probable at a stage at which the head had already differentiated itself. It cannot be decided whether, after an initial destruction of the tooth Anlage (and of the associated nerve?), the gill Anlage stepped in for it, or whether something wrong developed from the first. However improbable the first alternative may seem, it would nevertheless find its parallel in the heteromorphotic regeneration, observed by Herbst, of an antenna in place of a crustacean eye removed *together with the ganglion*. Similar cases, though not yet experimentally analysed, exist in the edible crab *Cancer pagurus* (claw in place of a maxilliped) and in *Cimbex axillaris*, a sawfly (foot in place of the terminal club of an antenna) (cf. Bateson).

Perhaps, however, the absence of the spiracle of the left side [sic] is to be interpreted as meaning that a fusion of it with the mouth opening has taken place, and in the process the gill Anlage has passed over onto the upper jaw: we should then have to speak not of a homoeosis but of a displacement.

3) The »malformation of the posterior body end« is in any case to be regarded as a defect formation; it could, however, owe its origin either to a primary inhibition formation or to a defective regeneration after loss of the hind end. The regeneration of the hind end has been demonstrated several times in fishes; since even in higher fishes the caudal fin itself is regenerated, it might cause surprise that it did not come to development in the lower selachian, and moreover in the embryonic state, if regeneration is supposed to be involved. Against this, however, reference may be made to the regeneration, obtained by Nusbaum and Sidoriak, of a conical outgrowth piece in young brook-trout embryos (*Salmo fario*), still provided with the yolk sac, from which *larger portions* of the hind body had been cut off. Now since in our shark the malformation already begins »roughly at the level of the posterior margin of the anal opening«, the regenerate would have to be reckoned from here, and not only from the beginning of the caudal fin. Corresponding operations to decide this question could well be carried out on those sharks which lay eggs.

4) The »two cases of duplicitas anterior« join the large number of similar cases which are observed among the bony fishes (cf. Schmitt), and which also occur elsewhere among the vertebrates, e.g. snakes, tortoises, gulls, roe deer, calves, humans, and among invertebrates, such as annelids, lobsters, etc. Four possibilities of explanation present themselves: a) two embryos began from the outset to develop and have grown together more and more with their tail ends; b) two embryos have subsequently fused in such a way that their anterior halves remained free in a definite symmetry relation; c) only one embryo has developed, which became double in its anterior end in consequence of a separation of the cleavage cells; d) an already developed embryo has suffered an injury of such a kind that in the anterior half it came to the regenerative formation of a second anterior end, or of the inner-lying complementary halves of the symmetrically split anterior end. According to the results of experimental morphology, all four cases can probably be regarded as possible; against the second, however, speaks the not-above-normal size of the shark double embryos. The greatest probability seems to me to attach to the third case: to this fit the double formations obtained by Bataillon (Plate V, Fig. 34) on *Petromyzon* eggs artificially (by alteration of the osmotic pressure). The first case, which Jan Tur, after measurements on numerous young embryos of various animal classes, holds to be the most frequent in nature, because even each of two primitive grooves that have been laid down is usually of full size, would confront us once more with the question whether the two Anlagen owe their first origin to a separation in the still uncleaved egg, or to the participation of more nuclei, that is to a phenomenon analogous to fusion. The fourth possibility offers the interesting problem up to what age the longitudinally split shark embryo would still be capable of replacing the lost part.

---

If, then, we cannot yet give definitive answers to the question of the origin of our various shark malformations, a wide field of work is nevertheless given for the application of the experimental method, and the great heuristic value of the malformations probably already justifies sufficiently their publication in this place.

*Translator's note.* This short two-author paper is one of the earliest publications from the Biologische Versuchsanstalt (vol. 22 of the *Archiv*, 1906): Grosser, an anatomist, describes four malformed spiny dogfish sent from the Trieste zoological station, and Przibram uses them to announce the Institute's »developmental-mechanical museum« and to argue that natural monstrosities are best read against experimentally produced ones. Term choices: *Anlage* is left untranslated; *Entwicklungsmechanik* = developmental mechanics; *Homöosis* = homoeosis (Bateson's term); *Defektbildung* / *Hemmungsbildung* = defect formation / inhibition formation; *Versprengung* (of an Anlage) = displacement; *Spritzloch* = spiracle; *Parietalgrube* = parietal fossa; *Fruchthof* = germinal area (Part I says the right spiracle is missing, Part II the left — the inconsistency is the original's, marked [sic]). Read today, the descriptions remain useful teratological case records, while Przibram's interpretation of the head fin as a displaced pectoral-fin Anlage that developed with secondary symmetry belongs to the Roux–Tornier regeneration framework of its time.
