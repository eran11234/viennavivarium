## I. Introduction.

Just as earlier chemical experiments had furnished me with the possibility of approaching the practical-chemical treatment of the problem of protective colouration in pupae, so the experiments carried out by Frl. Dr. Brecher and myself on the influencing of colour-producing ferment extracts in the test-tube by external factors have led to the uncovering of manifold relations to the most diverse fields of biology.

A sufficient experimental basis seems to me to be given for erecting a unified theoretical edifice that is capable of explaining the colour-dress of animals by means of known chemisms and factors.

In order to avoid extensive repetitions, I have arranged the division of the subject analogously to that of Part I, which describes the preliminary experiments on extracts, so that the sections of the contents correspond to one another and one finds, under the same section number in Part I, in each case the experiments on which the expositions in Part II rest. The bibliography of Part I is also to be used alongside, since I have omitted from the list of Part II the titles already given earlier.

A critique of the theories hitherto customary for the explanation of colour-dress is not intended in the present work. They have already been touched upon in passing in the introduction to Part I; a detailed comparison with my theory I should like to save for later, when further supporting material for the theses put forward will be available.

The working-through of the connections sketched hypothetically here, on experimentally accessible examples, has begun in our institute, and the results will appear as further instalments of the present treatise. I therefore request that, in reading this part, it not be lost sight of that what is at issue is the gathering-together of building-stones for a theory of animal colouration, and not a building completed in all its details.

## II. The Variability of Colouration.

### 1. Protective Colouration.

#### a) Light and Colour.

The ‘wave theory’ of light, at present generally accepted, rests on the comparison of light rays with mechanical vibrations. The propagation, reflection and extinction of trains of light behave as if wave-motions analogous to those on a vibrating rope or in a solid-fluid medium were taking place, yet in such a way that the media familiar to us of ponderable matter do not come into consideration as the vibrating particles. The hypothetical light-medium is customarily designated the ‘ether’.

If sunlight or the white light of another light-source is sent through a prism, a decomposition of the white light into coloured zones takes place, which are comprised under the name ‘spectrum’. The measurement of the wavelengths of prismatically refracted light yielded characteristic magnitudes for every saturated colour distinguishable by the colour-competent eye, so that the colours can be designated by means of wavelengths. The appearance of the spectrum rests on the increase of refrangibility with the decrease of wavelength. Of the visible rays the red have the greatest wavelengths, and are therefore deflected by the prism least from the original direction of incidence of the white light; there follow orange, yellow, yellow-green, green, blue-green and blue; violet, with the smallest wavelength of visible rays, appears most strongly deflected.

On both sides of the visible spectrum there lie further regions of rays which cannot be made noticeable by their direct differential action on our eye, but can be by other actions. These are the ultra-red rays, distinguished by heat, beyond the red end of the spectrum, and the ultraviolet, noticeable by photographic blackening, beyond the violet end.

If any body is struck by light, one part of the light rays may be thrown back, another may penetrate through, a third may disappear in the body without appearing again as light. (Finally, fourthly, light that has disappeared may reappear in the form of less refrangible rays: ‘fluorescence’.)

These portions are mostly more or less different according to their wavelength, and therefore determine the colours of objects visible to us.

If a body reflects all visible kinds of rays of the mixed light uniformly, it appears to us white at high, grey at low light-intensity.

If a body lets all visible kinds of rays through uniformly, we may designate it water-clear.

If a body absorbs all visible rays, it appears to us black. These three limiting cases are seldom completely realised, since on the one hand not all the light comes to reflection, transmission or absorption, and on the other hand not every kind of ray undergoes the same treatment.

If one kind of ray of a particular wavelength alone is reflected while the others are absorbed, the body appears to us in that colour which corresponds to the particular wavelength.

If a transparent body is concerned, the transmitted light may agree in colour with the reflected, but may also deviate from it (dichroism).

When reflection, or absorption and transmission, of rays of particular wavelengths takes place at a body depends on the chemical nature of the body or on a merely physical structure of it.

Those depending on the chemical nature are designated body-colours, the others physical or apparent colours. The former have their causes in molecular structure, in that certain atomic groups and atomic bonds favour vibrations of particular wavelength and suppress others (cf. Ley, 1911).

The physical colours can come about in any translucent substances whatever as a consequence of the formation of layers of very small thickness, adequate to the wavelengths of light. They rest on the interference of the light rays reflected from the layer-boundaries with those directly incident. In reflection, namely, every ray suffers a path-difference. If this amounts to half a wavelength, the troughs and crests of the incident and reflected ray coincide, and so-called ‘standing’ waves arise. At all other path-differences the troughs and crests must at least partly cancel one another, and there results a weakening of the motion, an ‘interference’ of the light. Since the coloured components of white light differ by their wavelength, the path-difference of half a wavelength cannot correspond to the same layer-thickness for the different colours, and the body appears in that colour whose half-wavelength corresponds to the layer-depth.

#### b) Light- and Colour-sensitive Substances.

Under prolonged or strong illumination many chemical substances change their body-colour. If the colouration disappears in the process, one speaks of light-fugitive substances; most plant pigments show this phenomenon. Some chemical substances undergo changes even on brief exposure to light which make themselves known by the assumption of an altered colouration, like the well-known silver haloid salts used for photography. Their ‘light-sensitivity’ is occasionally so great that they find use for the detection of traces of light (Secerov) and serve for the measurement of even slight light-intensities.

The various zones of the spectrum exert an influence of differing strength on photographic plates or papers, in that under otherwise equal conditions the blackening increases more and more from red toward violet. This does not correspond to the relative intensities of the colour-zones in the spectrum, which rather, according to the bolometric measurements (Langley, 1882), possess energy-values that correspond approximately to the brightness appearing to our eye, and thus show the maximum between yellow and green.

That it is not light-intensity alone that is responsible for the differing action of the colours emerges also from the ‘phototropy’ (Stobbe, 1908) of the fulgides and other substances. ‘Phototropy’ consists in a reversible change of colouration: if, for example, triphenylfulgide is kept in the dark or under a yellow or red filter, it possesses an orange-yellow colour; on irradiation with white light it becomes brown; on interposition of a blue filter it becomes a deeper black-brown, although the absolute light-intensity must have been reduced by the filter. White light thus also contains rays which counteract the blackening of triphenylfulgide and which belong to the less refrangible half of the spectrum. If one lets these red-yellow rays act on the fulgide that has become black-brown, it changes its colouration back again to orange-yellow. That the black-brown fulgide absorbs the greater part of the red-yellow rays and changes in the process, but as soon as it has become orange-yellow reflects the red and yellow rays and changes no further, agrees with Grotthus’s law that only those rays are effective which are absorbed by the reacting substance.

The effectiveness of a coloured light thus in general ceases when the altered body has attained a colouration agreeing with the colour of the light; substances which change in this manner have been designated ‘light-sensitive in the manner of absorption’ [absorptionsmäßig lichtempfindlich] (O. Wiener, 1895). The sensitivity-maximum is always displaced somewhat, relative to the absorption-maximum, in the direction of the red end of the spectrum — ‘Vogel’s rule’ (Acworth, 1891). A yellow-green body with strongest absorption of violet will thus be decomposed most in the adjoining blue and, if the minimum wanders analogously to the maximum, will be altered least in yellow (or orange).

The addition of a substance which absorbs particular rays is able to raise the effectiveness of these rays upon another light-sensitive substance, to ‘sensitise’ it toward light of a particular wavelength. The ‘sensitiser’ will reflect other rays, and hence mostly appear as a pigment.

If a substance yields, on its decomposition by light, differently coloured products of which each is light-sensitive in the manner of absorption, then this substance will in its body-colour assume in each case a colouration equal to, or rather similar to, the impinging coloured rays. Such a substance, producing the colours of the illumination by body-colours, has been designated ‘colour-receptive’ [farbenempfänglich] (Wiener, 1895). Such substances were employed in the colour-photographic processes of Seebeck and Poitevin. The pictures, which cannot be well fixed, appear even when seen in transmitted light in the same colours as in incident light, since each of the coloured substances arising partly reflects and partly transmits all the non-absorbed rays.

In an entirely different manner is the reproduction of the surrounding colours to be explained in Lippmann’s fixable colour photography. Here, according to the theory given by Zenker (1868) already for Becquerel’s non-fixable colour photography, standing waves are generated by the acting light, through reflection at a mirroring back wall, in the very fine-grained, transparent photographic layer, at whose nodal points no motion, and hence also no decomposition of the silver salts, takes place. The spacings of these nodal planes will differ in accordance with the different wavelength of the colours. On removal of the silver chloride that has remained undecomposed in the wave-nodes there therefore remain points of precipitated metallic silver at precisely such layer-depths as, on incidence of white light, reflect that colour whose wavelength led to the origin of the antinode. The remaining light rays pass unhindered, so that on viewing the finished colour photograph from the other side, i.e. in transmitted light, the colours are supposed to appear precisely reversed (complementary). Since only interference colours are involved, the colours cannot be extracted as pigments.

On intense exposure of the unfixed plate there comes about the formation of so many deposition-layers that no passage of light can occur any more after fixing, all light-waves are reflected, and the plate appears white in incident light, and viewed from behind opaque, dark. On too slight exposure, insufficiently many antinodal points are formed for light to be able to be reflected from them after fixing; the fixed plate appears dark in reflected white light, whitish in transmitted. If the surroundings of a sensitive plate consist of a black surface, we might perhaps expect that this would under all circumstances act like darkness. That, however, is not always the case, since black surfaces set up in white light are indeed unable to reflect onto the plate any of the colours visible to us, but are able to reflect the ultraviolet rays not directly perceptible by us, which are photographically effective. Only when these have been absorbed by interposing, e.g., a solution of quinine bisulphate does the photographic blackening fail to occur, as in darkness. Such experiments have been carried out by Wiener, e.g. on the Poitevin leaflets, that is, on body-colours. But the consideration holds generally for photographic and other photic processes, that black and dark are not to be equated without further ado.

In chemical processes which proceed even without the action of light, it may look as though darkness could exert a positive influence on the velocity of the process. Thus the oxidation of sodium sulphate proceeds in the dark half as fast again as in violet light. This, however, rests on a retarding action of the violet rays, for in yellow-red light this oxidation is, relative to darkness, accelerated by almost a half (Trautz, 1906). The opposed action of more refrangible and less refrangible colours comes to light in many other reactions too, in such a way that the one group acts acceleratingly, the other retardingly, relative to the velocity in the dark. In this it is not always the same ones which bring about the retardation or which intervene acceleratingly; thus the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide is accelerated more than twofold in violet, and slightly retarded in red relative to darkness. Pyrogallol on oxidation assumes a red colour, and this process is accelerated in red light; perhaps every process is favoured by that colour which it itself produces, just as we have heard that, conversely, every substance is most readily destroyed by the rays it absorbs.

Alcoholic guaiac solution colours itself green to blue in white and violet light under oxidation, and yellow again in red light under reduction. Flower colours (mostly lipochromes) are not altered by ultra-red and ultraviolet, but are destroyed by the rays complementary to their colour: orange-yellow ones by blue, blue ones by red, orange and yellow, purple and rose-coloured ones by yellow and green.

After decolourisation of the impregnated papers in an atmosphere of sulphurous acid, the flower colour can be restored by the action of the colours complementary to the decolourising ones (Herschel, 1842).

Light of various wavelengths also acts in a peculiar manner on ferment reactions. Thus the catalase which decomposes hydrogen peroxide suffers, relative to darkness, an increasing inhibition in the order red, blue, white (Lockemann, Thies and Wichern, 1908), whether the reaction itself or merely the ferment before use has been exposed to these rays. Still more instructive are experiments in which yellow rays too were taken into consideration. While violet irradiation inhibits the effectiveness of salivary diastase, red-yellow favours it (Green, 1897) relative to darkness, and matters stand similarly with the irradiation of living *Porthesia* caterpillars, whose catalase content is increased in red-yellow relative to darkness, and diminished in violet and still more in white (Wo. Ostwald, 1908). Yet catalase extracts from the same caterpillars are said to be decomposed by red-yellow rays too more strongly than in darkness, though more weakly than in other colours.

With regard to the influence of light, the guaiac-blueing peroxidase of living *Porthesia* larvae behaves precisely inversely to their catalase: on not too long an exposure, violet light favours the increase of the peroxidase in a similar but somewhat weaker manner than white, while yellow light often even inhibits peroxidase formation relative to darkness.

Intense light exerts an analogous influence on peroxidase extracts too, only here violet tends to act even more strongly than white (‘positive’ light-action, Ostwald).

Weaker light exerts this ‘positive’ light-action only after longer duration; it is preceded by a ‘negative’ one, in which violet light acts inhibitingly relative to yellow, and mixed light relative to darkness; in fresh extracts yellow even promotes the effectiveness relative to darkness.

In striking fashion we (Przibram and Brecher, 1919, I) were able to confirm this behaviour in oxidases through their action on tyrosine. The experiments, carried out chiefly on the ‘tyrosinase’ of the honey fungus, *Agaricus melleus* [= *Armillaria mellea*], a fungus, permit the following theoretical consideration:

In freshly prepared extract the tyrosinase is in a state A, which gradually passes over into the fermentatively more effective state B when no light-action is added, that is, in darkness. With this transition into the active state, however, the tyrosinase approaches the third state C, in which it loses its activity. Since in the dark the transformation of state A into state B proceeds slowly, the whole quantity of state A is not converted into B simultaneously so long as the destruction of B into C is not taking place, and probably the reverse process runs at the same time, in which B is again partly converted back into A. But as soon as the destruction of B into C has set in, the equilibrium between the transformation of A into B and the back-transformation of B into A is disturbed, because through the elimination of phase B a more rapid conversion of A into B must occur without a corresponding possibility of back-transformation. There comes about a (periodic) replenishment of activated B-tyrosinase, but in an absolutely ever smaller quantity.

The blue end of the spectrum acts inhibitingly on the activation; at first the tyrosinase neither comes into effectiveness nor is destroyed. Gradually, however, despite the inhibiting action, the transition from A to B makes itself noticeable: the blue-irradiated tyrosinase, at first ineffective, enters the maximum of its action, at which much activated B-tyrosinase is already present but as yet no destroyed C-quantity.

Opposite to the blue rays, the yellow-green act acceleratingly on the transition of the A- into the B-state. Right at the beginning, after brief irradiation, the yellow-irradiated ones show the maximum of their effectiveness. But soon the B-state passes over into the C-state as well, and the yellow-pre-irradiated tyrosinase appears inactivated.

Through the rapid transition of the B- into the C-state, however, the equilibrium between A- and B-tyrosinase is again considerably disturbed; there is again a replenishment of B-tyrosinase, and the effectiveness of the tyrosinase increases somewhat once more.

At the same time the blue-irradiated one decreases in effectiveness again, because now the B-state has passed over into the C-state, only then to ‘regenerate’ once more by means of the B-supply consequent on the disturbance of equilibrium, and so forth. If we picture these apparently intricate phenomena to ourselves in the form of curves, it is easy to recognise in what order the variously pre-irradiated tyrosinases should colour tyrosine samples set up simultaneously. Conversely, from series experiments continued sufficiently completely and long, the forms of the curves could be determined more exactly according to the time which must be used for pre-irradiation in order to obtain the various orders of onset after illumination of different colours.

Since the action of tyrosinase on tyrosine consists in the calling-forth of colours, a certain agreement between the colours called forth by different irradiation and the colours of the irradiation is possible. By honey fungus, tyrosine is coloured first yellowish, then reddish, then violet, finally black. If the tyrosinase which is used for the tyrosine experiments after pre-irradiation is in the initial state, where its effectiveness after yellow irradiation is greatest, a yellow surrounding will contrast sharply with the black product, and likewise the other colours with the surrounding colours. But if the tyrosinase, after somewhat longer irradiation, is in the ‘reversed’ state, then the yellowish tones of weakly effective tyrosinase will now run parallel with the yellow irradiation producing them, the violet tones with the now more strongly effective blue irradiation, the reddish tones with the red irradiation holding the middle.

If this approximately homologous colour-state called forth by different rays is fixed, e.g. prevented from further change by withdrawal of oxygen, then the samples can retain the former surrounding colours of the tyrosinase or — if the reaction was still carried out in the same surrounding light — these can be preserved in the product (for green illumination, however, this will not hold if among the colour-stages of the reaction there is no green one).

#### c) Light- and Colour-adaptation.

The colour-dress of animals comes about in three ways:

1. through dissolved pigments which shimmer through the epidermis from the body-fluids or are visibly deposited in the tissues;

2. through fermentative origin of colour-bodies in the epidermis itself;

3. through physical structural colours in the secretion of the outermost body-layer.

For each of the three groups we can derive, by analogy with the light- and colour-sensitive substances known to us, an explanation of the possibility of adaptation to the colour of the surroundings which reckons exclusively with the properties of the substances and structures actually present in the animal body that could participate in such colour-adaptations.

But I should like to emphasise at once that in each individual concrete case it must first be demonstrated whether a colour-adaptation according to the colour of illumination really takes place, which chemical substances cooperate, and whether their peculiarities with respect to the reproduction of the surrounding colours really find employment in the living animal body for the animal’s dress. The experimental and theoretical treatment of concrete examples is reserved for the following publications.

1. As dissolved pigments there come into consideration for colour-adaptation above all the yellow-green lipochromes widespread among arthropods and cold-blooded vertebrates, which are known to be light-fugitive and to be colour-receptive in so far as they are destroyed least by the rays which they reflect — in which, however, in accordance with the law mentioned, the yellow rays lying somewhat toward the less refrangible end of the spectrum should act even better protectively than the yellow-green themselves. Thereby the colour-adaptation may turn out less well than one might expect from the similarity of the animal green and yellow-green pigments to those of the surrounding vegetation; e.g. butterfly pupae in yellow surroundings are coloured a finer green than in yellow-green. The orange lying beyond yellow exerts the same action as yellow-green; all other colours act de-greening, increasingly with their distance from yellow-green and with the intensity of the light; hence purple-red as the complementary colour of green, violet as that of yellow-green, and white as the greatest intensity will allow the least green to arise².

> ² Extract experiments carried out in the meantime, to be published later, have shown the preponderance of de-greening by *strong white light*.

2. As colour-forming ferments the oxidases come into consideration, which through oxidation of colourless chromogens lead to the formation of coloured substances — in particular the tyrosinase of the arthropods and molluscs (which in the vertebrates is perhaps, according to Bloch’s investigation, replaced by dopa-oxidase, just as tyrosine is by dioxyphenylalanine). The light-sensitivity of tyrosinase of animal provenance expresses itself, according to our experiments, in the same direction as that of the plant and other related ferments. At first the effectiveness is increased in yellow light relative to darkness and diminished in blue light, only however to turn into the opposite on somewhat longer action. The effect will thus consist in a diminution of the colour produced in yellow, an increase in blue. A medium colouration, as in darkness, is produced by neutral grey, in which neither blue nor yellow predominates, as well as by other surroundings containing neither blue nor yellow, or both colours in mutually cancelling proportions.

Since in intense sunlight the yellow rays have a far greater share in the white colour than the blue, it often acts in the sense of a diminution of the dark colouration. Conversely, diffuse skylight is richer in blue and especially ultraviolet rays (cf. Czapek, 1913, p. 38); with the decrease of absolute light-intensity there therefore comes about a preponderance of the blue end of the spectrum, until in black only ultraviolet remains as the effective component relative to darkness. The strong chemical effectiveness of ultraviolet suffices for dark colour-formation to an intense degree. Only with exclusion of the ultraviolet rays does a surrounding formed of black surfaces act like darkness or like a neutral grey standing midway between white and black.

3. As physical structures for the formation of adaptive colours there come into consideration the surface-secretions showing interference colours, which are distinguished by their metallic sheen. So far as is known to me, these physical colours stand in relation to surrounding colours in a single case only, namely in the so-called ‘gold pupae’ of some butterflies, which occur on shining, especially gold-gleaming substrates and can be produced experimentally (Poulton, 1887). Since the metallic sheen can hardly be traced back to the light-receptivity or light-sensitivity of metallic pigments, one might here most readily think of the formation of true colour photography; yet so far neither the exact connection of the metallic sheen with the particular metallic colour of the surroundings, nor the exact structure of the metallic spots, nor a light-sensitive substance is known. Experiments on *Vanessa* are planned for elucidation.

### 2. Mutation.

#### a) Temperature.

Every body is warmed by the light rays it absorbs — on which, indeed, the bolometric determination of the energy of the spectral colours rests.

According to the modern theory of heat, the warming of a body consists in the increased motion of small particles, the molecules. In the solid state, situated in a particular mutual arrangement, the motion of the molecules takes place in vibrations about a mean position of rest. If through renewed supply of heat the motion becomes so violent that the bounded circles of vibration are exceeded, the association of the molecules is loosened and the body passes over into the liquid state of aggregation, in which the mutual arrangement of the molecules becomes a constantly changing one. On still stronger rise of temperature, molecules are flung out of the body without having to return again into the liquid; there comes about the assumption of the gaseous state.

Besides these changes of the state of aggregation, changes within the molecule can also be brought about by supply of heat, which on the attainment of particular temperatures lead to a bursting of the molecule, to the union of molecules or to rearrangements of the atoms within the molecule, and are therefore able at this temperature to convert the body into one with other chemical properties. Those degrees of temperature which are necessary for the change of aggregation or for the transformation of substance are designated transformation-points. At the transformation-temperature itself at least two states of aggregation, if a change of the state of aggregation is concerned, or two chemical states, if a material transformation is concerned, must always be able to exist simultaneously. On renewed supply of heat beyond the transformation-point, the transformation of the whole mass is first completed before a further rise of temperature takes place. However, the occurrence of the transformation on each occasion is not always bound to exactly the same degree of temperature. This depends, besides on the momentarily prevailing conditions of pressure, humidity and so on, also on the previous history of the body. In complete rest, liquids remain liquid far below those degrees of temperature at which they would otherwise long since have passed over into the solid state, and similar examples can be adduced in great number from the colloids; thus the solidification-point of glue-jelly which one allows to cool from the liquid state lies lower than the melting-point which one has determined by warming the solid glue.

Chemical transformation-forms of the same chemical substance, too, can be coexistent within a wide temperature-interval, although they arise from one another through corresponding change of heat.

#### b) Temperature-sensitivity.

From the kinetic theory of heat the conclusion can, in my opinion, be derived that such processes as rest on the increased motion of the molecules or atoms undergo an analogous acceleration through homologous supply of heat, while other phenomena are either hardly influenced at all by heat, like mechanical motion and the wave-motion of light, or suddenly attain great effects through the reaching of transformation-points, like explosions, for instance.

This view furnished me with the explanation of the well-known van ’t Hoff rule, that a rise of temperature by 10° within the so-called ‘limits of comfort’ yields, for the most diverse chemical processes, the same acceleration lying between 2 and 3 — but which, as I have been emphasising for years, holds not only for chemical processes but also for purely physical ones, such as evaporation, elastic after-effect, under certain circumstances crystallisation too, which rest on the motion of the molecules and atoms through thermal vibration.

Substantially smaller is the temperature-quotient for pure light-actions, which evidently do not act through raising of the thermal vibrations, like the chemically effective rays of light. Of special interest in this respect are the experiments on the higher temperature-sensitivity of the red-sensitive substances pyrogallol and sodium sulphate, which yielded quotients of 2.4 and 3.5 (Trautz, 1906), for the action of the *red* rays is indeed predominantly a heat-radiation.

Substantially higher is the temperature-quotient when a transformation-point is in the neighbourhood. By ‘limits of comfort’ are really already anticipated those intervals which do not lie all too near the transformation-points. Now if this interval is so small that it does not comprise 10°, or at any rate not several 10-degree intervals, the determination of the 10-degree acceleration will not be possible without coming into the neighbourhood of the critical points. The ferments especially, which are mostly of very unstable structure, often decompose at relatively low degrees of temperature, and this explosion-like activation with a rapid rise of effectiveness is followed by an early exhaustion; the optimum is thus soon followed by the maximum of the action.

Of the pigment-forming oxidases, dopa-oxidase (?) possesses an optimum below 57° (Bloch and Ryhiner, 1917), tyrosinase perhaps a still lower one.

At 100° both are completely destroyed.

The corresponding chromogens remain unchanged on momentary application of these temperatures, but from 37° upward oxidise spontaneously, i.e. without necessity of a ferment, to reddish (tyrosine) or dark (dopa) tones, when the temperatures have acted sufficiently long, which is usually attained in 2 (or 1) days. This discolouration sets in spontaneously even at normal room temperatures (17°), and indeed in tyrosine it needs 6 weeks to begin, which for the 20-degree temperature-interval of 17–37° corresponds to a 10-degree temperature-quotient of 3. Within the ordinary temperatures, then, there lies no transformation-point for this process of pigment formation.

The lipochromes change only on boiling, and thus likewise have no transformation-point at the temperatures of life; concerning the influence of temperature on the velocity of their formation, as concerning their formation in general, nothing is yet known.

#### c) Temperature-aberration.

Heat intervenes in the colour-formation of the animal dress in three ways, namely:

1. through the gradual acceleration, in accordance with the quotient, of chemical processes with increasing degree of temperature;

2. through the occurrence or approach of transformation-temperatures, which play a great role especially in ferments; and

3. through still more indirect linkage of the colour with structures or organs which can themselves suffer change through heat.

The ‘temperature experiments’ on insects carried out everywhere in such numbers in recent years have yielded chiefly change of colouration in the imagines emerging from the pupae when the pupae had been exposed to various degrees of heat. These ‘temperature-forms’ constitute on the one hand gradually differing series of variations which increase in a particular direction with increasing temperature, but on the other hand, at extreme temperatures, so-called aberrations which differ by a leap from the nearest temperature-forms, but which can be identical for too high and too low degrees (Standfuss, Fischer etc., literature in Przibram, Exp. Zool. III, chap. VIc, 1910).

The similar action of highest and lowest temperature has also been emphasised with regard to colouration in the reptiles (Kammerer, cf. ibid.).

On the assumption that the colours of the insect imagines, just like those of their pupae, come about with the cooperation of the (tyrosinase-containing) blood — in which all investigators (Dewitz, Meyer, Urech, v. Linden etc., lit. in W. Biedermann, Farbe und Zeichnung der Insekten, Winterstein’s Handbuch der Physiol. III, 1657, 1914) agree — and that the colouring-up of other animal groups is likewise predominantly to be traced back to chromogens and ferments, for which indeed all the chemical experiments hitherto available speak (molluscs — Przibram in v. Fürth, 1901, Gessard, 1902, p. 1398, Weindl; amphibians — Phisalix, 1898, Bloch, 1916, 1917 etc.), we can now refer the variations and aberrations to the two first-named modes of action of heat.

1. Variations increasing gradually with the temperatures will have their cause in direct action of heat on the colour-forming process, in that, e.g., the velocity of oxygen uptake may be correspondingly raised. At the termination of the colour-forming process, which does not depend on temperature alone, the colour-stage just reached is fixed, and the result is therefore permanently different.

2. The aberrations, on the other hand, will owe their habitus and their sameness at extremely low or high temperatures to the failure of ferments, which in general are extensively damaged at temperatures near the freezing-point and above our body temperature (at 100° entirely destroyed). The nearness of optimum and maximum brings it about that strong extremes of colouration will coexist simultaneously; the similarity of the transformation-points in different ferments, that possibly several characters change simultaneously (‘mutation’).

3. Still more indirect linkages than fermentative ones are certain nervous conduction-paths, in which the change of nervous tonus through heat can lead to a colour-change (cf. Przibram, Exp. Zool. V, p. 23).

That structural colours too could be altered by heat through alteration of the structure is indicated by the possibility of altering the intimate structure of the wing-covers of beetles by boiling (Heikertinger, 1915).

Nobili (1827) already sought to trace back the greater splendour of colour of tropical animals to the greater colour-saturation of interference colours under raised temperature, observed especially in organic substances (p. 421).

### 3. Variation.

#### a) Reaction.

By radiant energy, as well as by the thermal vibrations, changes are called forth in the chemical structure of the bodies affected. These changes can persist even after the cessation of the effecting energy and make themselves known through reactions which the altered body yields with chemicals of known properties.

The universally known chemical reaction-states of acid and base, or acidity and alkalinity, are ascribed on the one hand to the activity of the hydrogen ion, replaceable by metals in its compounds, and on the other to the activity of the hydroxyl ion (HO).

#### b) Reaction-sensitivity.

The reaction-state has a great influence on all albuminous bodies. Stronger acids or bases always precipitate or denature them. At weak degrees of acidity and alkalinity the order of the precipitating strength of salts reverses according to the reaction-state.

In the typical oxidases, especially tyrosinase, the reaction-state apparently plays a great role for their activity. Strong acids and bases destroy it completely (v. Fürth and Jerusalem, 1907; Abderhalden and Guggenheim, 1908).

Slight degrees of acid accelerate and exhaust, slight degrees of alkali inhibit and protect the tyrosinase action (Gessard, 1901; Przibram and Brecher, 1919, I).

Alkalinisation to a just perceptible increase of the alkaline reaction can be brought about, besides 1. by direct addition of very weak alkalis, also 2. by the action of violet and blue rays (in our experiments described above), or 3. by warming (which, however, on exceeding the optimum produces a permanent inactivation; in other ferments too — Traube, 1914).

In consequence of the exhausting action of acid on tyrosinase, this ferment is active only when hydroxyl ions predominate. Active tyrosinase can therefore be regarded as a mixture of a peroxidase and a hydroperoxide (Chodat and Bach, 1903; Bach, 1906).

Acidification to a just perceptible increase of the acid reaction can be brought about, besides 1. by direct addition of very weak acids, also 2. by the action of yellow and yellow-green rays.

In strong white daylight the alkalinising action predominates by far (just as daylight in other photocatalytic processes too leads to the formation of alkali; cf. Neuberg and co-workers, 1914).

Even slight changes of the reaction-state betray themselves through the alteration of the ‘onset colours’ [Angehfarben] of tyrosine: alkalis shift the colour toward red-brown, acids toward violet-blue (our experiments 1919, I).

The former has also been observed for the manifold onset colours arising through addition of amino-acids to the chromogens (Chodat and Staub, 1907; Abderhalden and Guggenheim, 1908). By careful neutralisation with acids the brown colouration can be converted back again into the characteristic onset colour (ibid. and in our experiments).

Even tyrosine samples which have already set on in colour are, as our experiments have shown, shifted by addition of alkali in the direction of the less refrangible colours, and by distilled water, neutral salts and very weak acidification toward the more refrangible colours.

The colouration of the lipochromes, on the other hand, is little sensitive with respect to the reaction-state at normal temperature; but the known phenomena relate merely to the finished substance, as which indeed the melanins themselves are insensitive even to strong change of reaction. Experiments on the influence of the reaction-state on the formation of the lipochromes are not known to me.

According to a communication of the late coleopterologist Ganglbauer, the metallic colours of beetles can be altered by acids and bases; this statement is to be followed up further on the occasion of intended experiments on the origin of the metallic colours in insects.

#### c) Modification and Race.

If slight differences in the reaction-state suffice to act upon the colouring-up in different tones, if weak acids and bases find employment as ‘colour-hormones’, then the explanation is substantially eased for us of how it comes about that rapid change of colour-tone in one and the same animal species — from body-site to body-site, from developmental stage to developmental stage, from specimen to specimen, from race to race — is an ordinary phenomenon.

We need not think of many different ferments, many different chromogens, many different pigments; the presence of slight degrees of acid or alkali will be capable of lending different tones to the pigment formation from one chromogen by means of one ferment.

The absence of colouration, too, need not always rest on a decrease of ferment content, but may owe its origin to too strong a degree of the hormone.

Points of support for the agreement of the change in colour within one and the same animal specimen with the change in reaction-states will come up for discussion later, in the treatment of the anatomical distribution of colour.

Changes of the reaction-state at different developmental stages we shall be able to expect above all where one stage passes abruptly into the other, e.g. in the metamorphosis of insects. Thus there are statements that in some insects all waste products remain stored up in the body during larval life, only to come to excretion suddenly at the transformation (Sphegidae, Marchal, cited after v. Fürth, Chemische Physiologie, Jena, 1903, p. 297).

In this the reaction-state can hardly remain unchanged. We have begun direct experiments on butterflies which seem to confirm this view, in that more acid extracts are obtained from caterpillars ripe for pupation. Since at the same time, at pupation, the outer skin is cast off and a new colour-dress comes to formation, it depends very much on the degree of the change of reaction what colour the pupa will assume.

In the pupae with light-inducible colour-dress, violet and blue rays will act alkalinising, yellow and yellow-green acidifying. By the latter two colours the optimum of acidification is soon exceeded, so that the dark pigments come little to formation; on the other hand the violet-blue rays neutralise the acidified tyrosinase to optimal action and thereby bring forth the violet-black melanin. Very strong reflected white light seems to act hyper-alkalinising and therefore brings with it, besides other effects (lipochrome bleaching), red chromogen colour.

The non-light-inducible pupal dresses show a noteworthy connection between tyrosinase blackening, the depth of colour of their pupal skin and the usual abundance of light at their place of pupation, which will give occasion for a further investigation.

Here the colour, which in the light-variable forms changes from specimen to specimen, appears to be fixed, as it were, in a state which has nevertheless preserved the marks of its original dependence on light.

If, following the nomenclature now customary, we designate the light-variable pupal colourations as modifications, and the non-light-variable ones, transmitted unchanged by inheritance, as races, then we are now able to see how the same colour-variation can appear as a modification and as a colour-race: the modifying external factor, namely, brings about in the epidermis of the animal the same reaction-state which in the colour-race is conditioned by internal circumstances. This state may have owed its origin to the action of the same external factor; but it may also have come about in another way.

A colour-variation induced by external factors, e.g. coloured rays, will appear again in the offspring if the same external factor again acts on the same developmental state of the animal and it had meanwhile produced no lasting effect on the reaction-state.

A lasting effect will be left behind by the external factor the more readily: 1. the more uniform its mode of action was, for the alternation of differently directed action must, or at least can, bring about a shifting-back of the change once set;

2. the more strongly it had penetrated as far as the germ-cells, for since the factors of the future animal colours are to be sought in these, the alteration of them will be able to lead, by way of parallel induction, to the formation of analogous colours in the children as in the parents, even without the external factor continuing to act on the fully formed animals (cf. Przibram, Exper. Zool., III. Phylogenese; Umwelt d. Keimpl. I);

3. the better the germ-cells are protected from further external actions after acquisition of the induced character, the more constant all the factors acting in the interior of the body, such as temperature, osmotic pressure, salt composition, are (cf. Quinton, 1904, Woods, 1910).

The species best equipped against the penetration of external factors — these are the highest-standing ones — will suffer changes of colours and other characters (cf. Woods, 1910) only slowly and at the strongest degrees of action; their colouration will appear very fixed, and possible colour-varieties will present themselves not as modifications but as colour-races, in which internal causes have influenced the reaction-state and which appear in the germs without renewed impulse, precisely because the colour-giving animal juices have undergone a reversal of reaction.

## III. The Physiology of Colouration.

### 1. Respiration.

#### a) Oxidation.

As is well known, by oxidation is understood combination with oxygen, by reduction the splitting-off of oxygen.

The former is bound up with giving-off of heat, the latter with uptake of heat, in which this heat may consist in the transformation of radiant energy, as in the splitting of carbonic acid by the green plants. The assertion of an analogous process of oxygen release in butterfly pupae (v. Linden) was not confirmed in checking experiments (lit. see Biedermann, Winterstein); yet, given the similarity of the animal greens to the plant greens, it is not excluded that under certain circumstances, especially of illumination, reduction processes play a role in the origin of the colours, similarly as with green chlorophyll. In general, however, we see the appearance of colouration, just like the appearance of form (cf. Przibram, Exp. Zool. 4. Vitalität, chap. IX), bound to oxidations with giving-off of heat. In the uptake of oxygen the chemicals effective therein and the site at which the uptake takes place come into consideration.

#### b) Oxygen-receptivity.

As we have heard, the chromogens combine spontaneously with oxygen on sufficiently long action. This oxygen-receptivity proceeds with the assumption of colouration, and is quite especially accelerated by the presence of corresponding ferments.

The tyrosinase action on tyrosine is considerably promoted by the presence of hydrogen peroxide (v. Fürth and Jerusalem, 1907; Przibram and Brecher, 1919, I); the addition of iron salts promotes the blackening of tyrosinase suspension in sunlight, probably through the intermediate formation of hydrogen peroxide, which even without illumination favours the blackening by iron salts (O. Adler, 1914). The fermentative blackening of tyrosine, too, is said to be intensified by iron salts (F. Durham, 1904; Th. Weindl, 1907).

The promotion of pigment formation by hydrogen peroxide will, as is obvious, rest on the splitting-off of oxygen from the hydrogen compound and its use for the oxidation of tyrosine or of another chromogen. Now, however, it was formerly regarded as a widespread property of ferments to decompose hydrogen peroxide, so that the promotion of blackening in the presence of hydrogen peroxide and tyrosinase could be traced back to the decomposition of the former by the latter. Later it was believed, in consequence of the separability of a hydrogen-peroxide-splitting component, the ‘catalase’ (Senter, 1903), that this property could be denied to the other oxidases.

Catalase, however, is found in all extracts alongside the oxidases, and remarkable relations emerge between the mutual quantities under particular external circumstances and internal states of the animals from which the extracts are taken (Ostwald, 1908).

1. A diminution of the catalase content runs parallel with an increase of the peroxidase content (as peroxidase a ferment colouring guaiac tincture blue was used, but Ostwald himself states that the tyrosinase of insects is on the whole distributed parallel to the guaiac peroxidase).

2. The diminution of catalase (Lockemann, 1908) and the increase of peroxidase (Ostwald, 1908) take place more rapidly in white and violet than in yellow light and darkness (at low intensities, however, the light at first hinders the peroxidase increase, just as in our experiments the tyrosinase is at first more effective in yellow and darkness than in blue and white, whereupon the reversal to the strongest action in blue took place).

3. Potassium cyanide inactivates catalase even in traces (Senter, 1903; Ewald, 1907), while it has no destructive action on peroxidase (Ostwald, 1908). (A 0.2% solution, however, hinders the natural blackening of fly larvae ripe for pupation; on temporary sojourn the larvae can become dark brown without pupating — Dewitz, 1902.)

4. Iron salts inhibit the catalase action (Lockemann, 1908), while, as reported above, they are said to favour oxidation and accelerate tyrosinase action; but only low concentrations may be applied (v. Fürth and Jerusalem, 1907).

5. Sodium chloride acts inhibitingly on the catalase reaction, as also on the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide by light (Lockemann, 1908); on the tyrosinase reaction it acted in our experiments at first promotingly.

6. The catalase reaction undergoes retardation through addition of acid (Senter, 1903; Ewald, 1907); tyrosinase action was accelerated in our experiments by weak addition of acid, whereupon exhaustion follows. Weak bases behave conversely.

7. In contrast to the oxidising action of the peroxidases stand the reducing actions of catalase, which, besides hydrogen peroxide, converts e.g. oxyhaemoglobin into haemoglobin in a blood solution to which ammonium sulphide has been added (Ewald, 1917).

The relations of the catalases and peroxidases that have become known so far — among which latter tyrosinase is to be reckoned — I interpret as the expression of a genetic connection between these two ferments, in particular because the shift of their mutual quantities or effectiveness takes place not only in the living animal but also in the prepared extract (such a solution was already hinted at by Ostwald; perhaps he also weighed the following thoughts).

I have endeavoured to form a concrete conception without drawing in any unknown fact, by starting from the fundamental property, or rather the definition, of catalase as a ferment decomposing hydrogen peroxide.

Such an action we shall expect above all from a substance which is not saturated with oxygen; if on the other hand complete oxidation of it sets in, it will forfeit the property. If we conceive the peroxidase (tyrosinase etc.) as an oxidation-state of the catalase, we shall thus be able to trace back the gradual disappearance of an extract’s capacity to decompose hydrogen peroxide to a progressive oxidation of the ferment. We then obtain the explanation for the 7 points adduced:

1. The peroxidase content will become the greater, and the catalase content correspondingly the smaller, the further the oxidation of the ferment has advanced (one can express this mathematically: in the equation *m* catalase + *n* peroxidase = *x* ferment, peroxidase is to be replaced by catalase + oxygen, hence *m* catalase + *n* catalase + *n* oxygen = (*m* + *n*) catalase + *n* oxygen, and *m* + *n* = constant).

2. The oxidation of the ferment will, just as we know it otherwise in oxidation processes, be accelerated by white and violet-blue light, hence increase of the peroxidase (tyrosinase) content to a stronger degree than in darkness and in yellow. The reduction of the ferment, however, or at least the prevention of further oxidation, will rather be favoured by the less refrangible rays, by analogy with plant respiration; hence under these rays the catalase will be increased at the expense of the peroxidase (tyrosinase).

3. Since the supply of oxygen is reduced by traces of potassium cyanide (cf. e.g. lit. in Przibram, Exp. Zool., IV. Vitalität), the oxygen set free from the hydrogen peroxide can suffice only to convert the catalase into its oxide, without free oxygen being able to escape again in bubbles; in the oxidised ferments, in consequence of their lesser oxygen-avidity, sufficient oxygen will still be able to be given off to the chromogen even in the presence of potassium cyanide.

4. The oxidation-favouring iron salts will favour the oxidation of the ferment likewise.

5. In the same manner acts sodium chloride, which is known in other connections too as a promoter of oxidative processes in living beings (cf. lit. in Przibram, Exp. Zool., IV. Vitalität, chap. V, 1a, 1913).

6. Acids will, through their hydrogen ions, occasion a binding of the oxygen set free in the hydrogen peroxide, and hence retard the catalase reaction; as regards the tyrosinase action, on the other hand, the analogous action of the hydrogen ion on the oxygen of the ferment will first activate the latter and thereupon damage it through reduction.

Strong reducing agents, such as phenylhydrazine, titanium chloride, sodium hydrosulphite and sodium bisulphite [Natrium bisulfurosum], destroy dopa-oxidase in 1% solution (Bloch and Ryhiner, 1917); in the presence of stannous chloride melanin formation fails to occur (Samuely, 1902).

Bases will, through their hydroxyl ions, bring about no binding of the oxygen set free, and hence not retard the catalase reaction; nor will they at first bring about any activation of the tyrosinase, but rather, through their hydroxyl ions, bind any hydrogen ions present and hence be able to protect the ferment from their accelerating action.

7. The oxidising action of the peroxidases, tyrosinases etc. rests on the giving-off of loosely bound oxygen, the reducing action of catalase on the oxygen-avidity of the ferment robbed of oxygen. In the presence of a chromogen that is itself oxygen-avid, tyrosinase therefore always unfolds the haemoglobin-reducing activity. Since strong inorganic oxidising agents acting through excess oxygen, e.g. potassium bichromate, oxidise tyrosine directly (cf. Abderhalden and Guggenheim, 1908), tyrosinase will perhaps turn out to be the dioxide of that ferment which as the sub-oxide [Oxydul] represents the indirect peroxidase and reduced catalase.

#### c) Site of Respiration.

Through the fermentative oxidation of the chromogens, the question — formerly eagerly discussed in the histological literature (cf. v. Fürth, 1904) — of the site of origin of the pigment deposited in the skin has been brought toward its solution.

In the insects the escaping blood is often rapidly blackened by the oxygen of the air; here, then, chromogen and tyrosinase circulate without finding in the interior of the body the oxygen necessary for melanin formation, and only through subsequent access of oxygen at the surface — whether through the stigmata, or, as in the butterfly pupae with radially branched chromatophores, through fine pore-canals traversing the cuticle — does the formation of the melanins come about at their definitive site of deposition. The oxidation, the actual tissue respiration, thus takes place in the colour-bearing cells themselves.

Similar conditions are offered by the epidermis of the vertebrates (in which, according to Bloch and Ryhiner, 1917, dopa-oxidase is supposed to take the place of tyrosinase), while as oxygen source mainly the red blood functions. Here, however, we do not yet know whether the oxidative ferment circulates in the blood or whether it is localised in particular regions, for which the demonstrations so far of blackening at quite restricted sites of the tissues and cells seem to speak. For the circulation of the chromogens speaks the demonstration (Bloch and Löffler, 1917) that Addison’s disease, which on failure of the adrenal function brings about a brown colouration of the whole skin, rests on the increased supply of a pigment precursor and not on an increase of ferment.

The chromogens themselves are, as I have demonstrated in the experimental work on tyrosine, so receptive of oxygen that they rob the red blood corpuscles of their oxygen, as emerges from the transformation of the oxyhaemoglobin bands into the reduced haemoglobin band — a process which is quite especially accelerated by the presence of tyrosinase.

For the origin of pigment at the surface of the body, the nearness of pores may also be decisive, which are capable of carrying off outward the waste products resulting from the oxidation, especially carbonic acid, ammonia and water, and hence keep the equilibrium-state of the chemicals favourable for the renewed uptake of oxygen.

The question of the site of respiration leads us, for the first time in the course of these theoretical discussions, to the further question of the sources or sites of origin of the three factors active in pigment formation: the ferment, the chromogen and the oxygen.

The last is the best known to us as to its provenance, since all animals obtain it from the outer air, the air-conducting respiratory organs are familiar to us, and the giving-off of oxygen can easily be demonstrated.

More doubtful already are the sources of the chromogens. It is known that these, e.g. tyrosine (and dioxyphenylalanine), arise in living beings as decomposition products of protein. They can be demonstrated in them by means of the corresponding oxidases; but we do not know whether they arose on the spot or were brought there by circulation. By analogy with other biological phenomena it might perhaps be assumed that in the invertebrates no localised source is present, while in the vertebrates particular organs are entrusted with the production of the chromogens, while the same or other organs also watch over the correct quantity of them in the circulation. One might here think of the adrenal, whose loss of function, as we have heard, conditions a flooding of the body with melanin, which is to be explained from increased chromogen that is not, as normally, converted in the adrenal into adrenalin.

Entirely unknown to us is the source of the ferments which are used for pigment formation. In the butterfly pupae the blackening, after the casting of the caterpillar skin, runs from behind forward, which one might perhaps at first wish to trace back to a spreading of the tyrosinase from behind forward. But the supply of oxygen, which takes place chiefly from the stigmata of the abdomen, makes it more probable that this factor is decisive for the temporal sequence.

Attempts to arrest the colouring-up by varnishing, immersion in water (Dewitz, 1902; Megušar, 1906) or ligaturing (Frings according to Urech; Urech, 1897) can furnish no decision, since with the prevention of the access of oxygen the pigment formation fails in any case, whether the ferment be present or not.

These experiments furnish only the proof of the oxidative nature of the whole process of melanin formation. Recently we have been able to demonstrate in a very simple manner, by corking samples with tyrosine plus tyrosinase, the ineffectiveness of the ferment in the absence of oxygen, while for dopa-oxidase the same has been proved by a more exact route, namely by a nitrogen atmosphere (Bloch and Ryhiner, 1917).

In fishes too, as in other animals (cephalopods — Hofmann, 1907; frog — Biedermann, 1892), anaemia has as its consequence a lightening of the affected part, which is usually regarded as a balling-up of the pigment cells. The same effect is produced by pressure exerted locally on the skin. The effective factor is in both cases lack of oxygen, which acts directly on the pigment cells, independently of the central as of the peripheral nervous system (v. Frisch, 1911).

### 2. Heredity.

#### a) Catalysis.

As catalyses are designated chemical processes in which the presence of particular substances acts acceleratingly on the decomposition or formation of substance.

In this the ‘catalysts’ have only the role of mediating the transfer of the components of substance, without themselves entering into the final compound, so that there is no necessity of particular quantities of the catalyst for the production of particular amounts of the substance to be formed. Yet most catalysts lose their catalytic capacities in the course of use, or even merely of time. With freshly prepared catalysts very small quantities often suffice to achieve large conversions of substance, in which case the particles of the catalyst must bring about, one after another, the transfer of many particles of the substance to be converted.

As inorganic catalysts act colloidal metals; catalytic action is shown also by the organic, likewise colloidal, ferments, whose chemical composition is still unknown to us.

#### b) Mixing and Unmixing.

If we add to one and the same solution simultaneously two different ferments of equal strength, three effects can occur:

1. The ferments do not influence one another in their action, which therefore comes to expression uninhibited in the reaction products.

2. The ferments influence one another in such a way that they contest the substances to be converted, so that each exerts only half the action.

3. The ferments influence one another in such a way that their actions cancel one another.

In our experiments with mixing of tyrosinases which had been made different by opposite irradiation, we were able to realise the first two alternatives.

1. A strongly blackening tyrosinase was not impaired in its colour-action by a non-blackening one.

2. On the other hand a tyrosinase which stood just at the limit of its capacity for action achieved, with the corresponding one produced by complementary irradiation, only half the depth of colour.

The 3rd alternative, namely the cancellation of the action, is supposed to come about in the formation of antibodies after injection of tyrosinase into warm-blooded animals, and would be explicable by complement formation (Gessard, 1902; doubted by v. Fürth and Jerusalem, 1907). These processes have no interest for the theme of animal colour-dress treated here, but we do have to keep in view the possibility of an inactivation through the presence of a second ferment.

That really no mutual influencing of the tyrosinases had taken place in our experiments follows also from the fact that from freshly mixed tyrosinases drops could be taken which unfolded entirely the action of the one or of the other tyrosinase, the cases distributing themselves according to the probability of a certain quantity of particles of the one or the other tyrosinase falling to each sample.

#### c) Dominance.

Since most animals arise from the mingling of two germs, the paternal and the maternal gamete, their colouration will likewise be dependent on two sides. Which parental colour can come to light will depend on the mutual behaviour of the mingled genes or bearers of characters, which in the united gametes, now designated zygotes, occasion the colour formation.

The pigmentation of the germs is usually not yet different according to the future colour-dress, so that the later colours come to light only in the course of ontogenesis.

Yet components of the later colour formation can often be demonstrated in the germs, so in particular the presence of the oxidative ferments occupying us here (cephalopods — Weindl, 1907; amphibians — Ostwald, 1907; also birds — Herlitzka, 1906).

In the ferments we have before us, as it were, extractable potencies of the germ-cells.

For the mixing of colour-races various modes of inheritance have been found, whose common features shall first be discussed (cf. lit. Przibram, Exp. Zool., IV. Phylogenese incl. Heredität). The colour-genes appearing side by side in the zygote do not influence one another, but separate again at the formation of new germ-cells, the grandparental genes combining in the grandchild zygotes according to probability (Mendel’s rule of segregation).

The mixed zygote of the child generation can come into effect in the animal body in three ways:

1. The colour of one parent comes forward completely unweakened, ‘dominant’, that of the other recedes entirely. Then in the grandchild generation there is, to three animals with the dominant colour, one with the recessive, because the two combinations composed of different colour-genes, the so-called ‘heterozygotes’, just like those composed of two dominant genes, show in the grandchild body the dominant colour alone.

Dominance is explained by the property of ferments of being able to convert larger quantities of substance, because they do not enter quantitatively into the compound. If both parental genes bring along a chromogen, but only the one a colouring ferment, this will nevertheless be able to convert the double quantity of chromogen into pigment, and the depth of colour will become the same as in the pigmented ‘homozygous’ parent.

2. Not in all cases does the colour of the one parent appear unweakened; rather, the first generation may also show an intermediate colour of both parents; the segregation of the grandchildren then analogously shows the intermediate colour in the two heterozygotes alongside one homozygotic combination of each pure parental colour.

We could imitate these cases in our experiments with tyrosinase mixture when the colouring ferment used had been diluted just to the limit of its effectiveness. It then suffices, indeed, for the conversion of a particular quantity of tyrosine, but not for double that; hence the latter remains for a longer time only half coloured. With rapid separation of the mixed tyrosinases the combinations 1 strongly coloured : 2 medium coloured : 1 not coloured could be attained.

3. In some special cases nova appear in colour-crosses. Two white parents, for example, whose white is recessive relative to coloured animals, may yield only coloured young.

This phenomenon has been sought to be explained by the distribution of two factors of colour formation between the recessive-white parents. If, e.g., the one white were to be referred to the absence of the chromogen, the other to the absence of the ferment, the union of chromogen and ferment could yield colour (as in plants according to Wheldale, 1911).

According to our experiments this interpretation does not seem probable, because even the entirely colour-free albinotic and cave animals do not lack the chromogen; nor would it furnish any explanation for the appearance of ‘dominant’ white.

In better accord with the facts would stand the assumption of ferments which both act colour-inhibiting in consequence of their reaction-state, but owe this in the one case to too strong an acid, in the other to too strong a base. On union to an effective neutral state the colour-inhibiting actions would cancel and the colour come fully to light.

Dominant white (which Gortner, 1911, traces back to the inhibiting action of certain phenols) would rest on such a strength of the colour-inhibiting reaction that even on doubling of the quantity of substance to be converted the inhibiting action still outweighs the colour-action of the colour-competent ferment partner.

For the proof of this theory experiments on fowls are in progress. The theory put forward would also make plausible how colourations apparently lost over several generations can come into effect again, even from two recessive parents, since external factors are able to influence the reaction-state.

From the tracing-back of ‘dominance’ to ferment action there results the possibility of referring its absence to the absence of fermentative processes. We shall be able to expect that characters of non-fermentative nature show no dominance, but are strictly governed by the quantitative relations brought along on the part of each parent. Perhaps we can interpret in this way the repeatedly asserted intermediate position of the children with respect to lengths and other spatial magnitudes. Certainly the becoming-hereditary of the double cell-size after propagation of regenerated pedicels with double chromosome number belongs here (Marchal, 1909).

The same interpretation would admit the preponderance of maternal characters, especially in the first developmental stages, even in ‘merogony’, i.e. absence of the maternal nucleus (cf. lit. Przibram, Exp. Zool., I. Embryogenese), since here the quantity of substance in the plasma would come into consideration; on the other hand the ferment in the sperm substance could be more effective and favour the emergence of certain characters, e.g. of the colours, which arise fermentatively. The latter we see in the species-crosses of butterflies (cf. Przibram, Exp. Zool., III. Phylogenese).

Sex-limited inheritance too, which is observed rather often precisely in the colours, would have to be investigated from the newly gained points of view, since the reaction-state of the sexes may possibly be different and hence sometimes incompatible with the formation of particular colours.

### 3. Assimilation.

#### a) Synthesis and Cleavage (chemical equilibrium).

If several substances are present in a solution which are able to enter into reactions with one another without thereby being precipitated as insoluble bodies, then there will be present in the solution all combinations of the substances at all possible at the given temperature, and indeed in a particular equilibrium relation.

If one of these combinations or individual ‘phases’ drops out of reactive capacity from any cause whatever, the chemical equilibrium of the solution must be disturbed, and an increased production of the phases that have dropped out will take place until the original phase relation is re-established. With limited mass or supply it will finally come to the transformation of the whole quantity into the relatively most reaction-incapable phase, and to the exhaustion of the whole process.

#### b) Ferment-channelling (specific ferment action).

The dropping-out of a phase from reactive capacity can take place through becoming insoluble and precipitating at a particular temperature, or through crystallising out, or through formation of a suspension.

The products of the process can in all these cases be the same; the means applied need exert no influence on the chemical constitution of the stable phase that comes about.

It is otherwise with the ferments, which for the most part conduct the reaction in a given solution in a manner specific for each ferment, so that the resulting end-product is a different chemical body according to the presence of the one or the other ferment.

Even within tyrosinase we have seen that the modifications forced upon it have exerted an influence on the course of the reaction and in part also on the end-product of the chromogen oxidation.

And this influence lies in the same direction in which the ferment had been altered by the external factor.

Previous warming of the *Pieris* tyrosinase to 40° let the tyrosine remain at the red stage just as if the oxidation process itself had taken place at 40°.

Acidification of the tyrosinase acted in the direction of a blue, becoming basic in the direction of a red tyrosine onset colour, just as an acid environment deflects the process itself toward blue, a basic one toward red. In this the same tyrosinase state acts analogously on different chromogens — the best proof that the ferment really steers the reaction into a specific path.

It presumably does this through continued disturbance of the chemical equilibrium, in that it always converts into more saturated, less reaction-capable stages those phases which are themselves capable of further utilising the ions that can be taken up and transmitted by the ferment.

#### c) Animal Assimilation.

By animal assimilation is understood the property of the albuminous bodies characterising the animal body of producing their like again. Every species produces again the albuminous bodies of its kind in its body-cells in growth and regeneration, in germ formation and reproduction. Through the presence of a particular specific combination of substances, then, the continued production of the same is brought about. From the fragments of the protein complexes stem also the chromogens, ferments and pigments which are decisive for the colour-dress of animals.

The analogy with the specifically channelling activity of the tyrosinases suggests tracing back the process of assimilation in general to ferment action.

## IV. The Distribution of Colouration.

### 1. Anatomical Distribution.

#### a) State of Aggregation.

The mode of coherence of the small particles of matter situated side by side we designate states of aggregation. The aggregates can be divided either, according to the progressive degree of freedom of the particle-motion, into solid, drop-forming liquid and gaseous, or the greater or lesser regularity of the arrangement of the particles can be chosen as the ground of division.

If the arrangement of the smallest particles is a thoroughly regular one, so that the atoms follow one another in a definite alternation even without regard to previously existing molecular boundaries, one speaks of the crystalline state of aggregation. If molecules arrange themselves relative to one another without regard to the position of their atoms, but in a definite density of grouping, ‘dispersity’, one obtains colloidal or dispersoid aggregates.

Finally, aggregates without ordered grouping of particles are designated amorphous.

#### b) Form of Precipitation.

Which degree of arrangement of the smallest particles takes place is, especially according to the more recent investigations (P. P. v. Weimarn, 1914), dependent exclusively on the external circumstances, not on the specific chemical constitution of the aggregating substance. The particular chemism determines, indeed, the particular crystal form, but not whether the substance is to crystallise at all, or what degree of dispersion it is to show. Every body should be able to show, just as it shows different states with respect to the free mobility of its particles, also such with respect to their ordering.

Of special interest for the explanation of animal colour-dress is the property of the albuminous bodies and — as the experiments on tyrosinase–tyrosine samples cited earlier have demonstrated — of the melanins too, of precipitating on quite slight additions of acids, bases and even neutral salts.

The form of precipitation is mostly the colloidal, and here, as a special configuration, fibrous coagulation appears in fibrin, but also in our pigments.

This constitutes a transitional state between the colloidal and crystalline states of aggregation in so far as probably (Diesselhorst and Freundlich, 1916) the whole molecules, under the influence of external forces such as pressure or streaming, can in consequence of their elongated shape be coordinated to one another in a definite manner and be so fixed at the transition into the solid state. Since the elongated shape will have to be traced back to a definite atomic arrangement, the atoms too are then presumably ordered alternately, yet without breaking through the molecular association. This crystal-like order makes itself felt in the appearance of double refraction, e.g. in streaming vanadium pentoxide (Freundlich and Leonhardt, 1915; Diesselhorst and Freundlich, 1915, 1916; Freundlich, 1916), whereas at rest it is, like amorphous liquids, not doubly refracting.

#### c) Pattern.

According to the concordant histological findings, the colour-dress owes its toneless and dark tones predominantly to the formation of pigments in the uppermost skin-layers, whereas the green and yellow ones predominantly to the pigments, shimmering through, of more deeply situated skin-tissue.

The liquid lipochromes, circulating fat-dissolved in the body-fluids, seem to form no special precipitation-figures on their fixation into dry colour-deposits.

On the other hand, the pigments belonging to the melanin series often appear in the form of branched ‘chromatophores’, which can be brown, red, blue, violet or black, occasionally also white.

This branched form I should like, according to our experiments, to regard as a precipitation-form of the fermentatively oxidising chromogen, influenced by the streaming of fluid in the cells or intercellular spaces, the precipitation owing its origin to a slight change of the reaction-state. Hence a certain linkage of form and colour will appear, both being influenced by the reaction-state.

The pattern of the animal colour-dress will differ from place to place according to which factors come together to form a particular chemism.

These factors are:

1. Pigment-forming ferments: tyrosinase (possibly dopa-oxidase etc.).

2. Chromogens: tyrosine (possibly dioxyphenylalanine etc.).

3. Reaction-hormones: acids, bases, water, neutral salts.

4. Oxygen: in skin-pores, respiratory organs, blood-vessels, nerves (?).

5. Light rays of various strength and wavelength.

6. Heat of various degree.

7. Dissolved pigments: liquid lipochromes in the blood or fat, deposited ones in the subcutis.

Dark patterns arise everywhere where pigment-forming ferments are present in an active state, where sufficient quantities of chromogens are split off from the protein, where the right reaction-state leads to the precipitation of the pigment, where abundant provision is made for the supply of oxygen, where no hindrance or even a promotion of the tyrosinase action by light rays takes place, where the temperature optimal for it prevails.

The absence of any single one of these six factors can lead to the suppression or weakening of the dark pattern. The seventh colour-factor, the green and yellow lipochromes shimmering through the epidermis, will be entirely covered over when the dark pattern is strongly developed, otherwise come to light as ‘ground colour’.

1. Abundant melanin-forming ferments are present in the eyes of most non-albinotic animals; further in their dark skin-areas. The absence of the same ferments in the white skin-areas of piebalds and in albinos generally can be demonstrated by application of the corresponding chromogens as reagents.

2. Chromogens seem, by oxidases as colour-forming reagents, to be demonstrable everywhere at the surface of animals and also otherwise to be widely distributed in the body; with respect to their possibility of colour formation the thickness of the skin will play a role, since thicker layers will be able to yield deeper tones in consequence of the dark cells lying one above another.

3. Reaction-hormones will appear in various ways according to the functional or morphogenetic processes taking place at the body-sites concerned or in their neighbourhood: even the unavoidable carbonic acid as excretion product, the fluctuating salt- and water-content of the cell-parts, will be able to bring about the precipitation of dark pigments and occasion their fixation into ‘chromatophores’.

4. Oxygen supply takes place either directly from outside, as we see in the variable butterfly pupae the dark pattern bound to the surroundings of fine pore-canals of the skin (cf. Brecher, 1917), or the inhaled oxygen is conveyed to the blood and only from there back to the body surface. Then the nearness of the blood-vessels and their size or density will furnish favoured zones of the dark pattern, which had long since been asserted on the basis of comparative-anatomical investigations.

Shutting-off of oxygen by pressure or by impermeable paper laid on acts lightening on pieces of skin in fishes (v. Frisch, 1911).

5. Light strikes different body-sites with different strength; in particular the upper side is strongly distinguished from the under side in its enjoyment of light. That here a connection exists with the darker colouration of the upper side is certain in the light-variable colour-dresses of fishes (cf. lit. Przibram, Exp. Zool., V. Funktion, 1914). To the parallelism between the action of light on eye and skin in eye-possessing animals I shall return later on the occasion of the discussion of the visual process.

The colour-promoting rays in white light are, to judge by our experiments, those of the refrangible end of the spectrum.

For the formation of upper- and under-side colours of butterfly wings and the colourlessness of the parts of the hind wing covered by the fore wing, an influence of light has likewise been assumed (E. Fischer, 1902).

6. Heat comes into consideration up to about 37° as a favouring factor, from there on as a colour-inhibiting one, since it then damages the ferments. However, temperature rising above 37° brings forth red pattern through direct oxidation of the chromogens (pupae of *Pieris* — Brecher, 1917; analogous experiments on rats, *Mus decumanus* and *M. rattus*, demonstrated in the Zoological-Botanical Society at Vienna on 11 April 1917 — Przibram, not yet published). In warm-blooded animals the different temperature of body-sites may possibly have to be taken into account for the strength of pigmentation, but no such parallels are so far known to me.

7. The yellow and green lipochromes will deposit themselves as ground colour, in consequence of their fat-solubility, especially at the sites of fat-deposition in the subcutaneous tissue. Districts richly supplied with serum or non-red blood will, when spread out in sheet-like fashion so as to permit the yellow and green surrounding colours a vigorous action, lead to a deepening of the green tone, since the yellow rays favour the green lipochrome colour (white light bleaches them rapidly).

With respect to the distribution of the colours, some further phenomena can be cleared up to which no great attention has hitherto been paid, although they are particularly striking to our eye.

I mean the phenomenon of the frequent juxtaposition of quite dark and white places, the phenomenon of white skin-covering with black skin, and the phenomenon of black peripherally situated parts in otherwise white animals (acromelanism, or acroleucism — Haecker, 1916).

Quite dark places next to quite light places — ‘zebra striping’ etc. — will easily appear on one and the same animal if the hormone leading to melanin formation becomes so strong that it stands at the limit of compatibility with the tyrosinase: then minimal differences of the body-sites will be able to call forth either optimal melanin formation or complete failure of melanisation. An example is furnished also by the black-and-white summer or heat form of the map butterfly (*Araschnia levana-prorsa*).

We have here before us a process similar to that in the origin of quite dark and quite light individuals side by side under the action of strong factors which now just optimally reach, now already exceed, the limit of melanisation (*Leptinotarsa* — Tower; lizards according to Kammerer; in the fire salamander melanos frequently occur alongside albinos as the second twin).

The white skin-covering on black skin, as it occurs in the dark-eyed grey horse, in the polar bear and others, can be referred to an exhaustion of the tyrosinase through the melanin formation that has gone on strongly in the skin in consequence of its reaction-state. That this state would be the acid one is indicated by the possibility of blackening, with basic ammonia, a substance obtained from grey-horse hairs corresponding to the melanin-acid of the black horse (Spiegler, 1903); by the directly demonstrable more strongly acid reaction of albino skin-extracts according to experiments not yet published; and by the parallel with the Lepidoptera, in which, following the accelerating action of acid after precipitation of the melanin in the caterpillar, melanin formation in the pupa remains arrested if alkalinisation is prevented by yellow radiation.

The occurrence of black peripherally situated parts in otherwise white-clad animals, e.g. the Siamese cat, the Russian or Himalayan rabbit, some langurs and others, can likewise be referred to slight differences in the oxygen supply at such sites as stand at the limit of the capacity for melanin formation.

Of special interest is the regrowth of black hairs at shaved places of the Himalayan rabbit (Walther Schultz, 1915, 1916), because it shows that in fact a slight difference in accessibility to external factors, such as atmospheric oxygen, light etc., is capable of procuring black colour for an otherwise white-haired site. Experiments will have to show whether the supply of the ears, paws, muzzles etc. with oxygen is a lesser one (as Schultz is inclined to assume) or a stronger one (as I rather believe), and accordingly whether the black colour is owed to the suppression of a hyper-alkalescence or to the abolition of a hyper-acidity. The answering of the question of the oxygen supply of peripheral parts will also be able to give information about the black-and-white ear- and tail-tips of our brown hare, about the white tail-tip of the fox, about the black one of the stoat in its winter dress.

Concerning the distribution of the colour-forming substances in embryonic development extraordinarily little is yet known.

The penetration of pigment with the spermatozoon into the frog’s egg might well also represent an origin of pigment under the oxidising influence of insemination, whether the opening of a gap for external oxygen uptake or a fermentative contribution of the spermatozoon be responsible for it.

The formation of dark chromatophores on the yolk-sac of the fish *Fundulus*, which then, after development of the blood-vessels, migrate toward these and spread further along them (Loeb, 1893), agrees with the necessity of oxygen supply for pigmentation, especially since only circulating blood, not blood brought to rest by poisoning, exerts this effect. The migration could, like every chromatophore movement, be interpreted as disappearance of the pigment at the old site, new formation at the new. The cause of the first site of origin remains questionable.

A mosaic-like distribution of the substances in the first cleavage stages is suggested by those colour-races in which, e.g., the anterior half of the body appears coloured, the posterior uncoloured. If in the ordinary wild-grey brown rat the germ normally contained the active tyrosinase in the upper blastomeres and none in the lower, the normal race, wild-grey above and light below, could develop. If on the other hand only anterior blastomeres contained tyrosinase, a race coloured only in front could come about. Since variations of the partitioning of germ-portions in different directions are known to us (cf. Exp. Zool., Embryogenese and Vitalität), and races too can differ by different type of cleavage, here again a rich field for experiments offers itself.

Of interest too are recent investigations on the temporal sequence of pigment formation and the increasing strength of pigment with the activity of cell divisions and epithelial spreading (axolotl — Haecker, 1916). These two circumstances allow the easier oxygen supply to be recognised without difficulty as the common element, so indispensable for pigmentation.

### 2. Zoogeographical Distribution.

#### a) Climate.

The sum total of all external inorganic factors in their seasonal fluctuations is designated ‘climate’, in so far as action on living beings and rocks is concerned.

Every region of the earth has its ‘natural’ climate, which acts on the animal inhabitants as well as on plants and minerals, in so far as these are not withdrawn from the natural surroundings by the activity of man. Man and domestic animals live mostly under an ‘artificial’ climate. For experimental purposes we can produce ‘experimental’ climates by deliberate combination of particular factors (cf. Przibram, Künstl. Klima f. biologische Versuche, 1910).

#### b) Climatic Zones.

The natural climate of any place on earth is determined on the one hand by its general geographical position, on the other by the particular circumstances of the locality.

The general geographical position is characterised by the distance from the earth’s poles, the so-called geographical latitude, by the bounding by land and sea, and by the vertical elevation above sea-level.

The particular circumstances of the locality consist in the formation of the landscape, the distribution of waters, the prevailing vegetation.

The earth has been divided into zoogeographical zones, which can be characterised by their general geographical position and far-reaching differences of their faunas.

Within these zones analogous ‘stations’ [Standorte] are met with, which resemble one another in the particular circumstances of the locality and also allow a certain similarity of their inhabitants to be recognised.

Since in the present discussion we are concerned only with the colours, we can confine ourselves to the example that in colour-dress the inhabitants of analogous stations resemble one another.

The causes of the different climate in the different zones are the position relative to the sun as the heat- and light-giving source, and relative to the oceans as reservoirs of moisture and air-currents.

The same external factors influence the climate of the stations, which differ from one another by greater or lesser seclusion against one or several of these very factors.

As such ‘stations’, partly closed to the external climate but for that exposed to further special compensations through heating, exclusion of strong illumination and so on, the dwellings of men and the stables of domestic animals may be regarded. Here too the effective factors of the ‘artificial’ climate are light, heat, humidity and composition of the atmosphere.

In the application of ‘experimental’ climate it is likewise these factors which we can alter and control at will (cf. lit. in Przibram, Exp. Zool., III. Phylogenese, 1910).

Since we have by now already repeatedly set forth the action of the four named groups of factors on the origin and preservation of the colour-forming substances, I refrain from a renewed juxtaposition of factor and chemical colour-effect in this section, and will in what follows attempt at once to sketch the connection resulting between climate and colour-dress when the influence of external factors on chemical colour formation is taken into account (cf. Tables A and B).

#### c) Local Colours.

In a Table A I have put together the regions of zoogeographical distribution with their characteristic dress-colours and factor-quanta.

A further Table B gives at the head of the vertical columns the external factors that come into consideration for pigment formation, with the colour-effects achieved by them according to their degree of action. The horizontal lines of the same table, which repeat the zoogeographical regions, indicate the direction of colouring-up which, according to this very mode of action of the factor-degrees on the pigments known to us, would be expected in the animals of the zone concerned.

In order to understand the general result of the agreement between the expected colouring-up (Table B) and the observed (Table A), let us start from the colouration familiar to us of our Central European subregion (I, 3), which is distinguished by a ‘grey-brown’ or ‘wild-grey’ colour of most inhabitants. If we look more closely at one of our brown animals, we mostly find that the dress is composed of dark, yellowish, reddish and whitish places in strongly mixed alternation. Indeed, even on a single hair or on a feather or on one field of a butterfly wing all the colours are found one above or beside another.

To this thoroughly medium state in the colouration of the animals corresponds the medium state of all the external factors.

As to light, the length of days and nights is neither immoderately different nor strictly equal, the strength of the light neither immoderately high nor very slight, the quantity of blue-violet or yellow-green light approximately equal; the temperature moderate, but variable according to the seasons; the humidity fluctuating and moderate throughout the whole year.

With respect to latitude-zone, factors and animal colouration, the New Zealand (IV, 4) and the Chilean (V, 4) regions behave correspondingly on the southern half of the earth, only that in the former a more green tone of the ground colouration suggests a more intense yellow-green action of the dense flowerless vegetation, in the latter the less yellow and more grey tone suggests a stronger action of the ultraviolet rays on the heights of the Andes and a less strong action of the yellow-green on the treeless pampas.

To the central field region is joined the northern forest region (I, 2) of northern Europe, Siberia and Canada: long summer days and long winter nights substantially reduce the alternation in degree of illumination; ultraviolet light is abundantly present, yellow-green — since the coniferous forests are coloured more blue-green, and the deciduous forests stand bare in winter — only little; relatively low temperature (with abundance of oxygen in the forests) and moderate humidity favour uniformly black-brown colours, such as most animals of these ‘fur countries’ show.

The strong light of summer, however, seems in some forms already to lead to a destruction of the colour-ferments, so that at the autumn change of dress white tones betray the winter dress.

The extreme development of this state is then shown by the boreal subregion (I, 1) with predominantly white colouration. Perhaps the low temperature too acts destructively on the ferments without attacking the chromogens themselves.

On the southern hemisphere the Antarctic (VI) corresponds in colour and climate to the boreal subregion.

On the other hand we clearly see the influence of extremely raised temperature, up to 34° in the July average, in the Mediterranean desert region, where a light red-yellow predominates; the ferment weakened by strong illumination and heat will be partly replaced by direct reddening of the chromogen at such rise of temperature. Extreme dryness at the same time prevents the appearance of blue-grey and blue-black tones. The same holds for the South African subregion (I, 4), which however, corresponding to a lower temperature, shows a less pronouncedly reddish dress.

A similar colour-dress, but tending more toward blue-grey with the somewhat lower temperature and greater humidity, is shown by the Hindustani subregion (III, 1), and the animals belonging to the Australian subregion (IV, 3) behave analogously, in so far as they do not, as inhabitants of luxuriant forests, experience the influence of these.

Quite especially the sight of the West African animals (subregion II, 1) has always captivated me: intensely red-brown tones are united at the peripheral body-parts with sharply separated black and white places into a magnificent effect. We can now hold the high temperature responsible for the red colour, the high humidity for the blue-black, the extreme light-effects for the white: it still remains to be explained why precisely the sharp separations occur side by side. I refer to the remarks made in the discussion of pattern on the frequent juxtaposition of black and white places under factors just exceeding the optimum. One might, especially in the vertebrates, also think of higher body temperature at the central parts, while at the other, less warm ones the external temperature would not be capable of forcing the reddening.

Above all, however, I believe I may see in the perfectly regular succession of days and nights of equal length a ground for the delimitation of uniformly coloured areas: the colour-direction produced by an external factor in its degree of action on the particular site will be able to be maintained the whole year long through the constant regeneration of the tyrosinase such as comes about with regular alternation of light and dark.

From the West African subregion the East African (II, 2) is distinguished by stronger, yellow illumination, whereby the colours of West Africa appear weakened; in particular more white and less black is present, and the yellow-red is less intense. The at times great dryness of the steppes is here to be taken into consideration.

In colour-distribution the Madagascan subregion (II, 3) is similar to the West African; but, corresponding to the lower temperature, the red is weakened to yellow-brown.

The tropical Himalayan subregion (III, 3) likewise resembles the West African climatically, and shows, corresponding to the temperature rising above 30° in the July average, vigorous red-yellow tones and also the black-and-white places. To the extraordinarily luxuriant yellow-green vegetation with its wealth of yellow rays corresponds the simultaneous emergence of green.

The remaining tropical regions are all distinguished by multicoloured, sharply separated colours: corresponding to the not excessive temperature, which even in the average of the hottest [month] remains below 30°, the yellow-red reminiscent of tyrosine does not emerge strongly; the luxuriant vegetation favours green, the high humidity black and blue tones. The latter emerge especially in the Ceylonese (III, 2), Indo-Malayan (III, 4) and Austro-Malayan (IV, 1); green and purple-red in the Polynesian (IV, 2), Central American (V, 1), West Indian (V, 2), and also blue in the Brazilian (V, 3) subregion.

Not all animals which live in one and the same geographical subregion are exposed in the same way to its climate.

If our exposition is to be on the whole correct, then within the region the station of an animal species will have to be of substantially modifying influence on the colour-dress.

The domestic animals, which live in stables kept warm, with raised humidity and substantially diminished oxygen content of the air, will be able to owe to the first factor an increase of the reds, to the second an increase of the blue-blacks, to the third a bleaching. In particular, however, the absence of the natural periodic action of light will prevent the formation of the regular colour-tones and their alternation on hair and feather, and the irregular action of new factor-degrees will be capable of calling forth irregular effects, as we see in the piebald animals, whose colours cannot even be fixed by heredity (cf. Przibram, Exp. Zool., Phylogenese, chap. V, 1910).

For the sharp delimitation of the patches, the juxtaposition of different colours and of differently coloured individuals, the relations already mentioned come further into consideration, which result from the heightening of the effect of extreme, constantly acting factors.

Even more than in the domestic animals, the absence of full daylight makes itself felt in those animals which leave their hiding-places only at dusk. The uniform illumination, unfavourable to tyrosinase formation through lack of stronger light-stimuli, the relatively lower temperature and higher humidity than by day, lend the nocturnal animals that blue-grey or yellow-grey dress, only slightly mixed with yellow, which we all know in the wild cat, the edible dormouse, the brown rat, many bats, owls and moths.

A whole series of gradual extinction of the influence of light we can establish in the cave-dwelling animals. Such cave-dwellers as house in self-dug earth-tunnels but seek out the sun by day are coloured in the central field region just like most of our other wild-coloured animals.

Grey to blue-black are the moles, hidden in the earth even by day, in which presumably the influence of humidity also makes itself noticeable, for the burrowers living in the dry sand-deserts are gold-gleaming, like the Cape golden moles, or even quite light, iridescent, like the Australian marsupial mole; in the hot sand of the Somali deserts live the quite pale rodents *Heterocephalus* and *Fornarina*.

Further steps toward decolourisation are then taken by the true grotto-dwellers, which are mostly coloured quite pale to yellow-red, in so far as no light at all penetrates to their abode. For this decolourisation, besides the lack of light, the low oxygen content is at any rate also decisive, perhaps also the constantly low temperature.

Species which dwell nearer the grotto entrances, and presumably also such as have not long penetrated into the grotto interior, as emerges from their capacity for sight, are black, or also, with partial destruction of the tyrosinase, red-brown.

In the deep sea too, with decreasing abundance of light, first multicoloured, then black, red and blue forms are said to be found in greatest number; the last where complete eyelessness has set in.

Finally the parasites too show, with progressive degree of withdrawal of light, a decreasing colour-tone, until at light-impermeable, oxygen-poor sites of the interior of their hosts’ bodies they are entirely uncoloured.

### 3. Colour-distribution in the Visual Field.

#### a) Colour-sense.

The lights and colours appearing to our eye admit of a division according to psychical qualities immediately given to us, though present in full number only in the colour-competent.

These psychical qualities by no means agree with the causes of the light- and colour-impressions ascertained on the part of the physicists. While the determination of the wavelengths of the spectrally obtained colours has ascertained an uninterrupted series, merely rising by degrees, we receive on viewing the spectrum four qualitatively different sensations: red, yellow, green and blue. In the violet adjoining blue we recognise a blue and a red component, and likewise in the spectral regions lying between the four fundamental colours named we pick out the two adjacent fundamental colours, whereas these intermediate colours are physically designated by a vibration-number just as unambiguously as the fundamental colours.

Conversely, white, which to the physicist always represents a mixture of rays of different wavelength, appears to us as a simple sensation. Black, which the physicist is accustomed to conceive only as a negation, as the absence of light-vibrations, is for us a positive sensation, and we need white and black as fundamental elements in order to be able, by combination of these sensations with the four fundamental colours, to designate all the lights and colours we encounter.

Only a few investigators have recognised in full sharpness that a separation of the physical and the psychological division of the colours is an unconditional requirement for a deeper penetration into the psychology and physiology of the colour-sense (Hering; Mach; Preyer; Wundt; cf. Walter Przibram, 1898, with lit.).

Thus the physiological optics at present dominant (Helmholtz, 1896), which has taken its starting-point from three fictitious specific nerve-energies (Young, 1807), is nowhere capable of furnishing a corresponding physiological foundation for the psychical quality-relations of our light- and colour-sensation.

It would lead too far to enter at this point into a detailed critique of the colour-theories hitherto.

On the basis of the mechanisms and chemisms necessary for photography in natural colours, the attempt has been made in more recent times to gain a farther-reaching elucidation of the visual process (Zenker, 1867; Darzens, 1895; Raehlmann, 1906; Przibram, Exp. Zool., V, 1914).

According to this the retina represents for us a light-sensitive plate, which through the fineness of the grain and the formation of a light-reflecting layer (tapetum according to Darzens; boundary layer of the outer segments of the cones according to Raehlmann) can be colour-sensitive. During the photochemical visual process, standing light-waves form according to the Zenker–Wiener principle, which precipitate decomposed substance as reflecting particles in the antinodes and leave undecomposed substance at the nodes, which in consequence of the different wavelength of the colours must lie at different depths of layer in the retina. The order of magnitude of these layer-spacings agrees in fact with that of the rods and cones of the retina. The cones, formed from fibres of different length, will thus dip into different layer-depths as antinodal planes of the various coloured standing wave-trains (Darzens), or be capable of taking up correspondingly different excitations at different heights of their horizontal platelets (Raehlmann). The rods are not distinguished by different length of their fibres, and therefore come into consideration for the general light-sensation; this assumption is supported by the absence of the cones in the presumably colour-incompetent nocturnal birds and their frequency in the insectivorous, presumably colour-competent birds and in the fovea centralis, the most colour-vigorous part of our eye (according to Darzens).

Besides the advantages of the colour-photographic theory of our visual sense that have been made known so far, I now wish to point to a series of further phenomena which, formerly traced back to psycho-physiological circumstances, can far better be referred to the physical properties of colour photography.

1. The approximation of the two ends of the spectrum in colour-tone is explained by the approximate doubling of the wavelength of red relative to violet, since the nodal planes produced by the latter approximately coincide with every second one of red, and hence will meet with similar cone-lengths.

2. The impossibility of seeing two complementary colours, red–green, yellow–blue, at one and the same point of the visual field corresponds to the opposed layer-position of the nodal planes of their standing waves (in the colour-sensation of the pairs I am able to recognise nothing opposed; red appears to me just as different from blue or yellow as from green): the layers spared by the one colour are destroyed by the other; the formation of specific standing waves fails to occur, and only the cone-activity yields grey.

3. Most strikingly the colour-changes of after-images agree with colour photography (first observed at Trieste in 1901). As we have heard, a photograph in natural colours reproduces the correct colours only when the light falls in from the same side from which the producing light had acted; if on the other hand the picture is viewed from the other side, in so-called transmitted light, all the colours appear complementary.

Now one obtains the same reversal in after-images when one removes the external light by closing the eyes or by looking toward a dark object, whereupon, as is known, internal light now appears. This admits only of the interpretation that we now get our photogram into sensation in transmitted light; contrasts cannot be at issue in this phenomenon, since the colour-tone of the dark object viewed is immaterial for the outcome, so that the exclusion of the external light constitutes the essential thing. On re-admitting external light — looking at a bright object — the original colour of the after-image returns again without regard to the colour-tone of the object. The repeated alternation of looking at dark and bright with the same after-image convinced me that the coloured fading of after-images is also not in play.

4. The retinal red, or Kühne’s visual purple, absent in the cones (e.g. fovea centralis) and present in the rods of our eye, can be regarded as a ‘sensitiser’ for yellow-green, which also in fact decomposes it most strongly (Przibram, Exp. Zool., V, 1914), and explains the brightness-maximum in this colour of the spectrum for our eye (a similar thought has since also been expressed by Loeb [1915], namely that the visual purple represents a photosensitive substance with the sensitivity-maximum at λ = 536 μμ according to Trendelenburg’s determination).

5. If the action of the sensitiser decreases with falling light-intensity, the cones, not equipped with it and serving the colour-function, will contribute relatively more to the sensation, hence green-blue will be able to be sensed better: here lies the maximum for low light-intensity (the dark-adapted eye; alongside which certain colour-blind persons, as ‘light-weak’ eyes, and likewise the eyes of many lower animals, where possibly the sensitiser is lacking, would have to be placed; more on this in section b).

6. With very strong reduction of light, only the direct activity of the light rays, effective without sensitiser, comes into consideration any longer; red can no longer be distinguished as a colour from black, blue remains brightest and longest visible: ‘Purkinje’s phenomenon’.

7. Since the strongest effectiveness, as we have already mentioned earlier (p. 203), is exerted not by the rays exactly complementary to the absorbed light rays, but this maximum tends to be displaced toward the less refrangible end of the spectrum (Vogel–Acworth’s rule), we shall expect, at optimal sensitiser-action of the visual purple, the strongest brightness displaced from yellow-green toward yellow-red: in fact the brightness-maximum wanders in strong light as far as orange (A. König, 1891; Helmholtz, 1896). In the absence of visual purple with presence of colour-sense, the absence of Purkinje’s phenomenon will be to be expected (cf. in the following section c on the fowl).

The colour-photographic colour-theory by no means excludes the correctness of the principles of other colour-theories, if one modifies these according to the actual conditions.

Nothing stands in the way of accepting Young’s idea of the different kinds of nerve-fibres which are supposed to transmit the different colours; only one must in doing so consistently apply the law of specific sensations, with which according to Helmholtz agreement is supposed to exist. White must not suddenly arise out of three colours; rather the specifically different white-sensation must have its own nerve, which precisely (according to Darzens) leads to the rods. We must also ascribe a nerve to black, which however could be identical with that for white, since we really sense white and black as the extremes of sensation of the same series of sensation. Finally, in place of three fibres for the colours at least four must be assumed, since besides red, green and blue we decidedly sense yellow too as specifically different.

With this we arrive at Hering’s three complementary pairs, the individual colours of which he regards as opposed states of one substance characteristic for each pair.

In the visual purple we can see the sensitiser for the white–black process: white would come to sensation through alteration, black through re-formation of it. If an oxidation process really underlies the fading of the visual purple (Valentin, 1878), I should like to set this to the account of the oxygen set free by illumination, for the eye undergoes acidification in the light (Lodato, 1902). White would thus correspond to a reduction or dissimilation process, black to an oxidation or assimilation process of the actual black–white substance.

The recently confirmed sensitivity of ferments to the yellow and blue rays, in which these exert complementary actions, now permits us to conjecture, for the supposed yellow–blue substance too, the substrate in tyrosine-like chromogens attackable by ferments under the influence of the colours. Yellow would correspond to acidification or reduction, thus to the dissimilation process, blue to alkalinisation or oxidation, the assimilation process.

Analogously a red–green substance could act, which, say through a greenish sensitiser (lipochrome- or xanthophyll-like substance), would be suited for dissimilation in red light, for the opposition of red and green seems in the sensitivity of the ferments to be in itself less effective than that between yellow and blue.

Yet here the light-sensitivity with respect to the behaviour of complementary colours still requires further experiments, and it would certainly not be excluded that there are still more visual substances differing from one another by steps, such as the ‘step theory’ of colour-sensation (Wundt, 1887) wishes to assume.

#### b) Light-reactions.

If we wish to find again in animals the specific colour-sensations of man, we are dependent on reactions which we are able to establish objectively, since the animals can of course give us no information about their subjective visual perceptions.

Movement or other reactions of animals occurring in response to coloured illumination may not without further ado be referred to perception of the colour as such; rather, further analytical experiments are required in order to exclude the sole effectiveness of the brightness altered along with the colour.

In this, regard must be had to the variability of the relative brightness of different lights when the general intensity of the light-source is altered. As we have already had occasion to say, with decreasing light-strength the greatest brightness for our eye is displaced from yellow toward green. Because the maximum of light-sensation thus lies in green or even blue-green in our dark-adapted eye, which then finally is no longer able to distinguish the colours, as well as in certain categories of colour-blind persons, total colour-blindness was inferred (by Hess; lit. and critique, cf. Przibram, Exp. Zool., V, 1914) for those animals which show maximal movement-reactions toward green or blue-green light: cephalopods, arthropods and fishes are accordingly supposed to sense only differences of brightness of the light, but not colours specifically. This conclusion, which seemed to me too far-reaching on formal-logical grounds alone, is unjustified for the reason, if no other, that not only the totally colour-blind but also the merely red-blind person shows the brightness-maximum displaced toward green: at least the distinction between yellow and blue or, more cautiously expressed, between a ‘warm’ and a ‘cold’ colour would have to be conceded.

With this would agree the trainability of the bee to yellow and blue, which does not hold for red and green (v. Frisch, lit. in Przibram, Exp. Zool., V. Funktion, 1914; disputed by Hess, who however, according to Buttel-Reepen, 1916, worked with dark-adapted bees and hence obtained totally colour-blind states of the eye).

As in man, we can conjecture a blue–yellow substance in tyrosine-like chromogens, which under the influence of light-sensitive tyrosinases are able to change in two directions.

On the other hand, in the nocturnally living arthropods perhaps only a substance related to our visual purple, suited only for white–black discrimination, appears in the visual red of the rhabdomes, such as has been described in the moths, nocturnal beetles and crustaceans as a substance bleaching rapidly at death (Carrière, 1885), and which the day-living ones presumably lack because of rapid decomposition in the light.

In birds and reptiles, in the absence of visual purple, yellow-red coloured droplets of fluid are found placed in front of the retinal elements, which serve as colour-filters and are supposed to make the sensation of the cold end of the spectrum impossible, so that, e.g., grains in the blue spectral region would not be pecked up by fowls (Hess, 1907). Since fowls, however, can be induced by training to peck up the grains in blue (Watson, 1915), the cause of their normally leaving them lies presumably in the unusual linkage of blue and edible grain. The red filter, however, a physical sensitiser, will — inversely to the chemical sensitiser, visual red, which achieves a raising of the brightness in green — bring about a relative raising in red. As ‘physical’ sensitisers, interposed colour-filters have also been used in colour photography (H. Lehmann, 1906), in order to prevent the excessive prominence of certain colours.

Further training experiments on fowls (Lashley, 1916) have shown the absence of Purkinje’s phenomenon, and thus confirm the conclusion drawn by me above (section a, point 7) about the nature of this phenomenon.

Already in the example of the fowl we see that the turning of an animal toward a certain light-strength can be influenced.

Much more striking are the conditions in lower animals, which at first seem to be driven compulsorily either toward the light-source or away from it. Positive phototaxis, however, mostly passes over into a negative one with increasing light-intensity.

If, however, the raising of the light-intensity is undertaken by yellow-red rays, the positive phototaxis is strengthened; thus the yellow-red rays exert a ‘positivising’ action. Analogously, daphnias (Frisch and Kupelwieser, 1913) migrate away from a blue-violet light-source despite diminution of the total light-intensity, and are thus ‘negativised’ by the short-wave rays (similarly the nemertine *Lineus* — Minkiewicz, 1906).

If, then, one wishes to speak here of sensations at all — which, given the presence of well-developed visual organs, can hardly be avoided — at least two specifically different colour-groups must again be assumed.

Now, however, there is, apart from the kinds of light themselves, a series of agents which can likewise bring about a reversal of taxis.

1. Positivising are: α) mechanical movement (*Cypridopsis* — E. W. Towle, 1900); β) change of concentration (*Lineus* — Minkiewicz, 1906), besides γ) yellow-red (*Daphnia* — Frisch and Kupelwieser); δ) acid, alcohol, ether (*Volvox*, crustaceans — Loeb, 1906; *Hydrophilus* and *Dytiscus* — Wo. Ostwald, 1908).

2. Negativising are: ε) heat (*Polygordius* larvae — Loeb, 1906); ζ) sea-water (marine gammarids — Holmes, 1901), and perhaps fresh water (*Hydrophilus*, *Dytiscus* — Wo. Ostwald, 1908), besides η) blue-violet up to ϑ) ultraviolet (F. and K.) (*Balanus* larvae — Loeb, 1912).

The positivising action can be traced back to acidification, the negativising to alkalinisation, if we take into consideration the modes of action known to us of the individual factors on our light-sensitive system: chromogen–tyrosinase–oxygen.

1. α) Mechanical movement calls forth in all tissues of the animal counter-exertions which are linked with an acidification.

β) In the same way I conceive the action of a dilution of the sea-water or of a subsequent restoration of the sea-water concentration, both of which act only for some time, and indeed in the same manner (positivisation toward blue light).

γ) In the yellow-red produced by potassium bichromate the yellow rays are effective as acid-forming, as has emerged from our experiments on the honey fungus (‘post-yellow’).

δ) Alcohols and ethers also often behave similarly to acids in other chemical respects.

2. ε) Heat acts, according to our experiments and as is otherwise known, alkalinising on tyrosine and similar chromogens.

ζ) Sea-water as a rule reacts alkaline, and likewise most fresh water.

η) Blue rays favour, according to our experiments, the alkalinisation of the tyrosinase.

ϑ) The ultraviolet rays have the same effect, since they are to be regarded as the effective agent of the black surroundings, which act similarly to blue.

Through the juxtaposition undertaken of the positivising and negativising agents, the earlier, little-regarded investigations (Wo. Ostwald, 1908) on the connection between ferment content and phototaxis acquire a special significance:

1. Positively phototactic animals, e.g. just-hatched *Porthesia* larvae, sexually mature *Camptonotus* [= *Camponotus*] ants and occasionally water beetles and diving beetles, show high catalase content, no or only much weaker peroxidase reaction.

2. Negatively phototactic animals, e.g. mealworm larvae and occasionally *Cossus* caterpillars, showed conversely very low catalase but high peroxidase content.

Since in my view all the experiments can be explained by a ferment altered in its effectiveness according to the reaction-state, we arrive at the further parallel:

1. positive phototaxis and catalase increase are to be traced back to the action of acid (or reduction or hydrogen ions),

2. negative phototaxis and peroxidase increase to alkali (or oxidation or hydroxyl ions).

The ferment coming into consideration should, at least in the arthropods, be identical with tyrosinase.

At any rate the latter shows the same sensitivity to the reaction-state and is always present, since it plays a great role in colour-change.

#### c) Colour-change.

By a long detour, which has led us over almost all fields of biology, we come back again to the question of the connection between the colour-dress of animals and the changing colour of their surroundings.

We made at the outset the assumption that in the butterfly pupae an example is present in which the colour-dress is able to direct itself according to the colours of the surroundings independently of visual sensations.

In this assumption, supported by the experiments of earlier authors, there lay already enclosed the question of how a possible colour-sensation of the eye in other animals might express itself in colour-change. It has indeed long been known that in most cases of colour-change the eye plays a great role (cf. lit. Przibram, Exp. Zool., V, 1914).

1. With intact eyes many arthropods and fishes accomplish a rapidly occurring adaptation of their colour-dress to the surroundings by means of the ‘play of chromatophores’.

Here too the question was ventilated whether the agreeing colouration might not perhaps be traced back merely to perception of differences of brightness. In the little fish *Blennius* I have been able to convince myself that this interpretation is not able to explain the various colour-adaptations, since the fish kept in a glass clad in colour shows at all times of day, and thus at very different degrees of brightness, the same colour-tone as the surroundings. Recently, too, experiments have been made on flatfishes (Mast, 1915) with the holding-up of yellow paper strips, which led to the fish becoming yellow without substantial alteration of the general brightness.

2. This specific colour-adaptation is extinguished when the eyes are painted over black or extirpated.

However, by no means every influence of light on colour-change is eliminated by the blinding of the eyes. The blinded animals can be clad light or dark by change of illumination; yet in older amphibian larvae and fishes it has been observed that, contrary to the seeing ones, they become dark in the light and light in the dark. At the youngest stages the amphibian larvae react even when seeing just like the blinded older larvae, and thus have as yet no capacity for light-adaptation (are they blind?).

3. After long-continued keeping in complete darkness, or long life with the eyes removed, an increase of the dark pigmentation appears, and indeed also at such places where previously no dark chromatophores were to be seen. Finally, in both cases all dark pigment disappears, usually after a moult; yet some pigment remains in the eyes of the dark-kept animals.

4. These phenomena, to be designated ‘morphological’ colour-change in contrast to the fleeting ‘physiological’ colour-change, find their counterpart in the dark colouration of the pale, blind olm in the light, while the seeing fire salamander in black surroundings gradually loses its yellow spots.

All these so heterogeneous and partly paradoxical phenomena can be cleared up in a relatively simple manner if we regard the chemical reactions in visual sensation as refined and intensified, but in essence unchanged, repetitions of the chemical processes in the colour-change of lower animals.

1. In consequence of its adjustment specially set for the reception of light, the eye will in the same time convert a larger relative quantity of the light-sensitive chemicals, and in this way form a sink for the inflow of these chemisms, in particular also of the ferments. The substance decomposed by a particular kind of ray, which mediates to the eye precisely the colour-sensation concerned, will also be drawn from the rest of the body for replacement, and hence be diminished in the same sense; conversely, on increase in the eye, it will be more spared elsewhere too and hence show increased action. Unfortunately nothing is yet known to us about the source of our chemisms: here a wide, entirely uncultivated field still lies open.

2. If with blinding the tyrosinase-sink of the eye drops away, the direct action of light on tyrosinase activation comes forward, and the animals become dark in the light, and light in the dark, where diminution takes place.

This more direct influencing now seems to prevail in the amphibian larvae up to a certain age even without blinding, the eye not yet having taken up its function as a sink.

3. The increased tyrosinase activation leads, with very long duration, to an exhaustion which manifests itself in the complete bleaching of the blinded animals or those kept in the dark. In the latter case the eye again shows its preponderance in laying claim to the last still available tyrosinase for the purpose of retaining its pigment (*Fundulus* — Loeb; *Astacus* — Megušar etc.). This may, however, also be connected with the formation of the eye-melanin taking place at an earlier embryonic state.

4. If, conversely, no tyrosinase has been activated for a very long time, then on restoration of favourable conditions of activation, above all illumination (and abundance of oxygen), an extraordinarily vigorous melanisation comes about: the olm brought into the light assumes, after longer keeping, a blackening which, especially in the male sex, surpasses in thoroughness that of the relatives normally living in the light.

If no complete proofs can yet be adduced for these connections, a series of further indications is nevertheless at our disposal which will permit a further pursuit of the problem.

1. The eye seems in general to show a higher degree of acidity relative to the skin, which would speak for its suitability as a tyrosinase-sink, since higher degrees of acidity destroy this ferment and hence would have to alter rapidly the equilibrium of its metabolic cycle. The rapid destruction of tyrosinase in the eye probably also explains the difficulty of demonstrating ferments in the eye (cf. e.g. Bloch, 1917, who failed to find the dopa reaction in the eye).

2. Through the presence of seeing eyes the general respiration of animals is substantially influenced, and indeed heightened.

Besides this indirect action of light, however, it also exerts through the skin a heightening of oxidation, for in the light the respiration of skin and lungs is heightened relative to darkness even in blinded vertebrates (Moleschott and Fubini, 1878, with literature). To the eye there belongs in this just as great a heightening action as to the light through the skin, which again would agree with an enormous consumption of substances in the eye.

3. In blinded animals and in seeing animals kept in darkness the general oxidation processes are reduced (Moleschott and Fubini, 1878), which must also act inhibitingly on the production of activated tyrosinase.

4. The heightening of the oxidation processes in the olm brought into the light also makes itself noticeable in the intense reddening of the gills (Rusconi, 1837).

To my conception of the identity of the chemical processes in colour-change and in the action of the eye, two phenomena seem to stand opposed: namely, first, that the rapidly proceeding colour-change, and in many cases the morphological one too, is traced back to the contraction or expansion of ‘chromatophores’; and second, that in the butterfly pupae blinding is supposed not to lead to any alteration of the colour-adaptation.

I have made both objections to myself, and have thereby come to a re-examination of the two phenomena, the result of which yielded a confirmation of my theory.

If only expansion or contraction of existing ‘chromatophores’ were responsible for the darker or lighter colouration, it would remain incomprehensible how, e.g., stick-insects of different darkness could yield extracts of different darkness, how local shutting-off of oxygen produces local light patches in fishes, how, besides the extension of already existing chromatophores, an increase of them on blinding is also ascertained (pike — Mayerhofer; trout — v. Frisch, 1911), particularly intense in amphibians according to Megušar and the still unpublished experiments of Fischel, Dembowski and myself.

It would, however, stand in complete accord with the facts if the colour rested not at all on the expansion of the chromatophores, but on the formation of pigment in that kind of cell which is precisely chemically capable of pigment formation. The special branched form of the expanded chromatophores I refer to the precipitation-figures of the chromogens, which has been dealt with above.

Whoever might wonder at so rapid a production, at so rapid a destruction of pigments, I refer to the blue, diffuse pigment of the crustaceans, which appears in the dark and vanishes at once in the light — and which, incidentally, will also be drawn into the circle of our experiments — and to the visual purple!

The second objection makes it necessary to have regard to the manner of blinding, for it would after all be possible that the eye would function as a sink already through its presence, not only in its functioning in the light.

In favour of this would speak that the eye reacts acid in the dark too. For the frog direct experiments are available (Moleschott and Fubini, 1878): the carbonic acid excreted in the dark being in each case set equal to 100, intact frogs excreted in the light 125 parts, those with darkened cornea 115, those with extirpation of the eyes 111.

The mere presence of the eyes, whose cornea had been made entirely opaque by cauterisation half a year before, still sufficed for a relatively greater raising of oxidation in the light as compared with the eyeless frogs.

The painting-over or superficial cauterisation of the eyes, as had hitherto been carried out on the caterpillars (Poulton, 1887), may therefore for this reason not have been sufficient for the extinction of the colour-change.

In fact the colour-adaptation of the caterpillars can be prevented by a more thorough operation. On the experiments on *Pieris brassicae* Frl. Dr. Brecher will report later, for which reason I confine myself here to the mention of the fact, which now, as an elucidated and merely apparent exception, most beautifully confirms the rule.

In conclusion I should like further to point out that the copying, by seeing flounders, of a background composed chequerboard-fashion of dark and light fields — which until now could only be conceived as a telephotographic process of the pictorial impression in the central organ (Loeb, 1912) — can also be traced back to the experimentally ascertained peculiarities of tyrosinase or of a similar oxidase.

Since in the eye of the flounders alternating fields of greater and lesser consumption of tyrosinase arise, a juxtaposition of more or less inactivated tyrosinase will come about. If now more active and more inactive tyrosinase is conveyed back to the skin along the conduction-paths known for colour-change (cf. v. Frisch), then, thanks to the immiscibility of the tyrosinase states which persists for some time, a distribution over the skin can come about in which the more active and the inactivated state settle side by side and apart from one another, so that dark and light fields alternate. If the fields of the background pattern are smaller, the alternation of the tyrosinase states in the conducting fluid will be a more rapid one, and will also produce a more rapid alternation in the colour-pattern of the skin. If the fields of the pattern seen are larger, each of the differently active tyrosinase grades will occupy larger coherent quantities of fluid and, in acting on the skin, produce larger colour-patches, uniform in themselves but different from the others.

## V. Summary.

I. Since the colour-forming substances of the animal body show, in the test-tube too, a sensitivity to external factors analogous to that in the living organism, a biochemical theory of animal colour-dress can be derived.

II. 1. The adaptation of animal colouration to the colours of the surroundings takes place through light-sensitive ferments and chromogens light-receptive in the manner of absorption.

2. The aberrations called forth by extreme degrees of temperature rest on the temperature-sensitivity of the ferments (and chromogens), which remain fully capable of action only within a relatively narrow temperature range.

3. The modifications of colouration called forth by external factors rest for the most part on alteration of the reaction-state of the tissues, and an alteration of the reaction-state in the germ-cells in the same sense, in consequence of simultaneous influencing by the external factor (parallel induction) or through internal causes, leads to the identically coloured race.

III. 1. The pigments arising through oxidation are formed at their site of deposition itself, which is thus always itself the ‘site of respiration’.

2. The fermentative origin of most (all?) pigments of the animal dress facilitates the transmission from parent to child, since the ferments need be present in the germs only in very small quantity (potency) and are in fact demonstrable. Mixed ferments can be separated again without mutual influencing (segregation), and small quantities of strongly colouring ferments are able to convert even the double quantity of chromogen (dominance).

3. The colour-forming ferments determine the colour which the colourless chromogens have to assume, and in this way help to form again those substances which were present in the animal concerned (assimilation).

IV. 1. As a rule the yellow-green pigments belong to the deeper, the black, blue and red to the uppermost skin-layers; the former are dissolved, circulate or rest in fat-deposits; the latter appear in the dendritic-filamentous precipitation-forms characteristic of the chromogens (chromatophores) and call forth the pattern.

2. The specially predominant colours of the animal colour-dress in the zoogeographical regions, stations and in the domestic animals can be referred to the influence of the external factors, which act in corresponding degrees on the colour-forming substances.

3. The ‘colour-photographic’ theory of colour vision, developed by analogy with colour-adaptation, gives us an account of a series of otherwise unexplained phenomena of our visual sensation, brings the specific colour-reactions and phototaxis phenomena of lower animals into complete accord with the chemical processes, and clears up the role of the eye (as ‘tyrosinase-sink’) in colour-change.

*Translator’s note.* This is the theoretical half of Przibram’s two-part ‘Ursachen tierischer Farbkleidung’ (Arch. Entw.-Mech. 45, 1919), the companion to Part I with Leonore Brecher on tyrosinase and tyrosine extracts; it sets out the Vivarium’s biochemical theory of animal colouration — light-, heat- and acid/alkali-sensitive ‘ferments’, chromogens and lipochromes — from pupal colour-adaptation through heredity and zoogeography to a ‘colour-photographic’ theory of vision and of colour-change. Term choices: Farbkleid/Farbkleidung = colour-dress and Färbung = colouration; Ferment = ferment (the period word for enzyme); Angehfarbe = onset colour; Reaktionszustand = reaction-state; Fermentbahnung = ferment-channelling; Tyrosinasesenke = tyrosinase-sink; Przibram’s ‘Lipochrine’ = lipochromes; the ditto marks of Tables A and B have been expanded to the words they stand for, and the entry ‘braun’ printed across both humidity columns for the East African and Madagascan rows of Table B is shown as ‘— brown —’. Read today it is a speculative synthesis whose Lippmann-plate theory of colour vision and climate-to-colour mapping were not borne out, but its core claim — that pigment is formed by enzyme-driven oxidation of tyrosine-type precursors, modulated by acidity, temperature, oxygen and light — points toward the modern understanding of melanogenesis.
