## I. The Colour Types of *Dixippus morosus* [= *Carausius morosus*].

### 1. The colouristic differences in physiological and morphological colour change.

For more than 10 years the stick insect originating from India, *Dixippus* (= *Carausius*) *morosus* Br. et Redt., has been reared in Europe. All observers are struck by the diversity of the colourings in which these insects appear. The colour change occurs not only in different specimens of the same culture, but also very often in one and the same individual. And here it may again be a matter either of a physiological colour change, which the animals are wont to undergo at particular times of day, or of a morphological one, which is able to lead to the permanent assumption of a different colour dress. All these colour variations have become known chiefly in the much more frequent females, which mostly reproduce parthenogenetically, so that we need at first concern ourselves only with these (on males cf. IV, 1). We wish to distinguish three colour groups, namely brown specimens, green specimens and those which are neither brown nor green. Each of these groups shall in turn comprise three colourings, according to the increase of the dark pigment. In this way we obtain 9 colour types, of which 8 — those which appeared in our experiments — are to be seen on the accompanying colour plate. The 9th type, namely the lightest grade of the neither-brown-nor-green group, which was also known to me from earlier cultures, unfortunately did not appear in time on this occasion, so that it could not be reproduced along with the others. In the adjoining Table A an attempt was made to identify the colours observed by the various authors with our mode of designation, in which especially the coloured illustrations of Schleip and MacBride–Jackson rendered good service. According to the reports of de Sinéty, Meissner, Schleip and Dobkiewicz, the physiological colour change seems always to keep within one of the three main colour groups distinguished by us, so that a brown specimen may indeed assume variously dark shades, but cannot beyond that exchange with red or green, and so forth. Yet black colour seems to alternate both with dark brown and with red. We have ourselves made no experiments on the physiological colour change of the stick insects and must at once remark that our investigations always relate to colour types which were encountered side by side at the same time of day, not to the same colour type at different times of its physiological colour change. That this was really so will emerge from the presentation of the chemical differences (cf. I, 3).

A brief characterisation of our 9 colour types may not be superfluous for later identifications, since transitions between them, although rare, do occur, so that within each type there still remains a certain latitude which cannot be reproduced on the colour plate.

1. The lightest specimens are straw-coloured to ochre-yellow; on the upper side every greenish tinge is absent, below greenish places may occur. Since this form furnishes the starting point for a series of brown colour types, we have designated it as ‘brown 1’. In the physiological colour change it assumes, according to the statements of Sinéty and Schleip, darker brown shades towards evening.

2. There naturally follows here the somewhat darker colour type ‘brown 2’, which comprises hazelnut- to Havana-brown shades. Beneath the brown colour a greenish shimmer may show through on close inspection, especially when the brown colour appears applied not uniformly but rather in a mottled fashion.

3. Still darker is the colour in ‘brown 3’, which appears dark chocolate-coloured to bark-brown. Since all parts of the upper side are heavily coated with brown colour, there can no longer be any question of a greenish tone.

4. The colour type not represented on our plate is intended to include the colour varieties designated by Schleip (Pl. I, Fig. 3b) as yellow-red, by MacBride–Jackson (Pl. IV C) as yellow, by Dobkiewicz as light red. I have myself had the opportunity of seeing specimens in all transitions from yellow-free rose-red to strongly yellowish orange, so that the grouping of these colours under ‘red 1’ appears justified.

5. In the experiments utilised by us for the present work, of red pieces only dark red ones occurred, which showed an English- to Pompeian-red tone, which we shall designate as ‘red 2’ and, since ‘red 1’ does not concern us further, also simply as ‘red’. Such colour varieties are said to be able to become quite black in the physiological colour change.

6. Here, then, follows the entirely black colour type, which bears a dull neutral to coal black. In this type alone among all the colour types the inner sides of the fore-legs, so far as they embrace the head, are also suffused with black, whereas these places are otherwise carmine-red.

7. In complete contrast to the preceding type, every black or brown colour is absent on the upper side of the verdigris- to grass-green ‘green 1’. These pieces must also show no reddish or yellowish shimmer, in order to represent the type purely.

8. If the upper sides are suffused with brownish, often in an irregular manner, we are already dealing with ‘green 2’, which at first sight looks apple- to olive-green, until on closer inspection one distinguishes brown and green places side by side.

9. Again considerably darker appears ‘green 3’, which has occurred to us only rarely. On a moss-green ground a grey-brown marking stands out in mottled fashion. The animals appear grey from a distance; it is therefore possible that some of the colourings designated as grey by other authors referred to our ‘green 3’.

### 2. The histological differences.

The work of Waldemar Schleip has enabled us, without histological investigations of our own, to state the differences which exist in this respect between our colour types.

According to Schleip the hypodermis of *Dixippus* harbours three kinds of pigment, whose greater or lesser quantity is decisive for the colour varieties, while their mutual change of position conditions the physiological colour change.

The one pigment consists of very fine green granules, which lose their colour on treatment with alcohol. It does not change its position with respect to the hypodermis nuclei. Where it is present almost alone, in the green *Dixippus*, no physiological colour change has been observed either.

The second pigment appears in the form of roundish fields of yellow-red granules and possesses a capacity for horizontal migration, by means of which it is able to spread out and hence, in the physiological colour change, to make the animal redder. The yellow-red granules also give up their colour to alcohol.

The third pigment is a dark brown one, which according to Schleip is supposed to occupy the deepest position, that is, beneath the layers in which the other pigments are situated. Since, however, it can undertake a vertical migration, one finds it, with approaching darkness, also beside and, in the darkest colour varieties, even above the other pigment layers. For various reasons it seems to me more correct to regard the superficial position of the dark pigment as the original one: as Schleip himself adduces, de Sinéty found in a closely related phasmid (*Leptynia attenuata*) in the very young larva the pigment granules immediately beneath the chitin and only at later stages saw the reverse position. Then again, it would be strange if the phasmids behaved differently from the mantids, in which the brown pigments form the uppermost layer and are stripped off with the cuticle at the moults, so that the skins of the green specimens look wax-yellow, but those of brown ones smoke-grey. Although Schleip asserts complete colourlessness of the *Dixippus* skins, this does not hold for dark specimens, as Meissner reports to us: ‘I wish further to remark that the cast skins of the brown animals likewise showed a (mottled) brown colouring; on the other hand I always found the skins of the green specimens colourless.’ Since the moults mostly take place at night, the opportunity is given for the pigments situated in the uppermost layer to be cast off in the process. That the physiological colour change also takes place during the moult has been depicted by Schleip (Pl. 3, Figs. 26, 27), in which, to be sure, passage of pigment into the old cuticle is not to be seen.

For our present consideration this point of difference has no further interest. We now wish to discuss, on the basis of the histological information supplied by Schleip, the differences of our 9 colour types.

1. ‘Brown 1’ are specimens in which the green pigment is not present, and of the red and brown only little. Schleip considers it probable that in place of the green pigment there are grey granules, which are nothing other than originally green ones that have lost their colouring matter, but leaves it undecided whether the actually visible grey masses do not merely represent the plasma itself.

2. ‘Brown 2’ are specimens with very little green, but on the other hand more dark (and perhaps also yellow-red) pigment.

3. ‘Brown 3’ are specimens with very little, if any, green pigment, but on the other hand much dark (and perhaps also yellow-red) pigment.

4. ‘Red 1’ have no green, little dark, most of all red pigment.

5. ‘Red 2’ have no green, abundant dark, still more red pigment.

6. ‘Black’ have no green, by far predominantly dark, but also much red pigment (‘red 3’, as follows from the physiological colour change!).

7. ‘Green 1’ have abundant green, vanishingly little dark and red pigment.

8. ‘Green 2’ have abundant green, but also dark and yellow-red pigment.

9. ‘Green 3’ have green, much dark (no yellow-red?) pigment.

It is thus shown that by combination of the three cell constituents found by Schleip as formed pigments all our colour types can be derived, and that there should be no further, essentially different ones.

Of the three pigments the alcohol-resistant dark one is doubtless to be regarded as melanin; the two others, on account of their solubility in alcohol, can stand in no closer relation to it. Since the green and yellow-red colouring matters of insects mostly possess a lipochrome-like character, it was probable that the green and yellow-red colouring matter of *Dixippus* also exhibit such a character.

We were particularly concerned to trace the differences of the colour types back to chemical foundations. Therefore an investigation of the melanin-forming capacity of our colour types and an extraction of the lipochrome-like substances was undertaken, of which the following section shall treat.

### 3. The chemical differences.

On 21 December 1916, 6 female imagines each of the colour types accessible to us were picked out; of ‘green 3’ only 2 could be found. The stick insects were anaesthetised with ether and cut into pieces with a small pair of scissors, then mixed with so much 0.05% common-salt solution that 1 cm³ fell to 1 specimen, and ground in a porcelain mortar with quartz sand. The mixture was taken up in a straining cloth and pressed out. These press juices showed different colours according to the colour types to which they owed their origin. They could be applied with a painter’s brush to drawing paper and have retained their colour unchanged up to the present day (18 January 1920). On the colour plate the shades belonging to each colour type are reproduced after these natural water-colour samples. The adjoining Table B may spare a lengthy description. Already from this result it was clear that the different colour types could not rest upon the expansion or contraction of ‘chromatophores’ or upon a migration of elements remaining the same, but that chemical differences must be present, since in the pressed-out liquids the arrangement of the pigments could play no part; rather the quantity and combination of colouring matters must have been decisive.

In order to demonstrate directly the colour-forming potencies of the colour types, the press juices mentioned were subjected to a further treatment, in that they were mixed with an equal quantity of saturated ammonium sulphate solution. The precipitate which formed was filtered off. The filtrate (which we designate as Filtrate I) again had different shades according to the colour type (cf. Table B), but these now no longer corresponded to the generating type. As a rule the filtrates of the types with slight melanin formation (e.g. green 1) now darken more strongly afterwards, while in the others an exhaustion of this process makes itself felt.

On 27 December the Filtrates I were coagulated by warming to 90° and, by filtering off, filtrates were obtained which, however, showed the same yellow colour in all types. The coagulum was to be dried and dissolved in ether in order to separate out the lipochromes, but nothing more went into solution. On the other hand it had succeeded without difficulty in extracting from the original press residues, by means of ether, colouring matters which again did not show the same colour mixture in all colour types. As Table B reproduces, a distinct difference shows itself especially in this, that the three ‘green’ exhibit a greenish tone of the ether extract, while all the others have imparted an orange colour to the liquid (whether the more yellow tone in ‘red’ has significance is indeed doubtful, but it could rest upon a certain admixture of green masked by the intense red tone). The result shows that the green colour types are distinguished by the presence of a green lipochrome, which otherwise can be present at most in traces. The sameness of the ether coloration in the remaining colour types (apart perhaps from ‘red 2’) indicates that these are distinguished from one another not by a lipochrome but by another pigment. That can only be different strengths of melanin formation, as has indeed already emerged for brown 1 to 3 and black from the histological investigation (Schleip).

In view of the difficulty of attacking the melanins, a direct extraction of the same would be of little promise to attempt. A certain measure of the density of the melanin of the various colour types could still most readily be obtained from the aqueous suspension of our press juices. However, we are well informed by our earlier works (Brecher 1917, 1921, Przibram and Brecher 1919) about the mode of origin of melanin through the action of tyrosinase upon a chromogen present in the insect body (probably tyrosine), and for the purpose of obtaining tyrosinases the treatment with ammonium sulphate discussed had just been carried out. The precipitate so obtained was washed on 22 December in half-saturated ammonium sulphate solution: the filtrate (designated as Filtrate II, Table B) again showed different colours according to the type. The residue was dissolved in weak aqueous 0.05% caustic soda.

The opaque solution already indicated, by the rapid appearance of a black ring at the surface, that it contained tyrosinase. After filtering off the particles remaining in suspension on 23 December, various samples were set up with 1 cm³ of tyrosine and one drop each of the tyrosinase from the different colour types. If we consider Table C, which illustrates for us the results of such experiments (in which an increasing degree of blackening is designated by small Latin letters), it is shown that besides the strongly active tyrosinases of ‘red 2’ and ‘black’, notably ‘green 1’ is, at first surprisingly, equipped with a strongly active tyrosinase, while the brown colour types contain relatively little active ferment. A manifold repetition with tyrosinases from other specimens of some colour types confirmed this result. We shall return to its explanation later and here only point out further that the relatively great tyrosine blackening of ‘green 1’ still increases with time. The *colour types* are thus also distinguished by differences in the strength of their tyrosinase. There now remains one more chemical difference to mention, which relates not to the tyrosinase but to the chromogen to be converted by it, and which has already been described in an earlier work (Przibram and Brecher 1919, pp. 133, 175) (we repeat it here in Table D).

If the chromogens contained in Filtrate I are decolourised by heating and added to any tyrosinase whatever — it need not even be an animal one — then, during the melanin formation, figures appear above the surface of the slowly evaporating sample which are independent of the tyrosinase used, but are bound to the origin of the chromogen in such a way that with the chromogen of each of our colour types a particular formation, different from that of the others, comes to light (‘green 3’ could not be investigated). The cause of this phenomenon remains just as enigmatic to us as before, since we have had no opportunity for renewed examination. The distinction of the deposit formed above the different colour types rests on the combination of 3 pairs of properties, namely colour (blue or white), form (palisades or flakes) and density (coarse or fine). But, as the appended Table D makes clear, no relation can be established between the properties and the combinations of colouring matters known to us, of which each colour type appears to be composed. One might perhaps think that it could be a matter of growth forms of micro-organisms which had settled on the chromogens. Such fungi are indeed found frequently, especially on unboiled chromogens. In boiled ones they are seen more rarely; but I have occasionally seen such beside the figures in the same sample. They float, however, either on or in the liquid, or project partly out of it. But never does one find them in ring form exactly above the evaporating surface of the liquid. Nor are their growth forms and their colours different according to the chromogens. Rather, the figures give the impression of myelin forms or other semi-crystallised structures. Small differences in the composition of the chromogens, or other admixtures carried along from the different colour types, may then, as ‘solution partners’, influence the crystallisation. The blue colour I consider merely an optical one, in reality indicating a very thin layer. In any case, however the figures may come about, only chemical differences can be responsible for the appearance of the diversity.

The colour types of *Dixippus morosus* thus differ from one another in their chemistry in the following points:

a) the quantity of melanin formed (water suspension),

Which combinations of colouring matters our different colour types yield we need not explain once more, since one need only put the correspondingly coloured substances in place of the histological pigment granules. The two lipochromes mingled in the ether extracts can also be separated chemically if one treats the ether extract with ammonium sulphate (cf. Przibram and Brecher 1919, p. 179, serial nos. 378, 379). There then appear, above the water-clear lower half of the test tube, orange-red dots and above them green liquid, which in turn is bordered by black (melanin?) granules.

## II. The Influence of Illumination on the Colouring of *Dixippus*.

### 1. Experiments in reflected light.

In the summer of 1916 a large quantity of eggs was laid by the *Dixippus* reproducing parthenogenetically in the Institute, from which all the young hatched with brown colour, as the earlier observers of *Dixippus* cultures have also stated.

For testing the influence of reflected light of various colours on the final colouring of the imago, 15 young each were distributed at random among gauze cages whose inner walls were covered with paper of various colours. Only the front wall, facing the incident south light, remained uncovered. 15 different coloured papers came into use, and in addition, as controls, an uncovered cage in the light and one in darkness. On the appended Table E the colour designations are entered at the head of the vertical columns, while at the beginning of the horizontal columns our 9 colour types are recorded. The animals were fed with *Rubus* leaves, which were stuck on their twigs into transparent bottles; the foliage was lightly sprayed daily. It is surely unnecessary to communicate anything further about the very easy rearing of the stick insects; remarks on it are to be found in the works of our predecessors. In some of our cages all 15 specimens reached the imaginal stage; in all cages more than 2/3 of the inmates lived to see it. On 29 September more than half of the surviving animals were transformed (119 of 224), and their colour type was entered in the record sheet. On 18 December the remainder were also transformed and the recording was concluded. The result of the two days has intentionally been reproduced separately in Table D, because, as we shall see, a certain significance attaches to the difference of date.

We discuss first the result of the first recording day alone, and indeed we wish to examine each colour type with regard to the surroundings in which it predominantly made its appearance.

1. ‘Brown 1’, our lightest type, appeared in all 6 *Dixippus* transformed on 29 September in the light yellow, straw-coloured cage, further once each in light lilac, in light green, in the lemon yellow which is somewhat darker compared with the straw colour, in white, and twice in light neutral tint. In darker cages it occurred nowhere, just as little without covering or in darkness. Dobkiewicz obtained such pieces in yellow and green surroundings (probably employed in light shades).

2. ‘Brown 2’ occurred in most of the *Dixippus* from light lilac, in all 6 from sky blue and all 9 from dark yellow (roughly gold cinnabar), further in part in light green, lemon yellow, pink, white and grey, singly in brilliant yellow and black. This too agrees with the statements of Dobkiewicz, who reared medium-brown *Dixippus* in lilac, blue and white.

3. ‘Brown 3’ appeared in 6 (of 7) *Dixippus* from a medium violet, in 3 (of 4) from dark violet, in 4 (of 6) from bright red, in all 6 without covering, further in half of those in brilliant yellow, in a third of those in black, and singly in lemon yellow and pink. Meissner had reared dark brown specimens under a ‘thick, blue’ wire grating.

4. ‘Red 1’ is nowhere present on 29 September. (In a later experiment, whose details are not communicated further because it turned out too incomplete, a pink *Dixippus* occurred beside ‘red 2’ in a black box placed in the sun.)

5. ‘Red 2’ was shown by only 2 specimens from brilliant yellow surroundings. In Dobkiewicz red appeared singly in control cultures.

6. ‘Black’ are 6 (of 10) *Dixippus* from black surroundings, further 2 from medium violet, 1 from dark violet, 1 from bright red and 1 from brilliant yellow. De Sinéty (p. 138) obtained behind red glass only black *Dixippus*. A dozen specimens reared in a box with opaque walls gave the same result, which surprised de Sinéty, since cave animals are after all wont to be pale (p. 242). After the experiments of MacBride, Jackson and ourselves, to be discussed later, it seems to me probable that Sinéty did not work with sufficient exclusion of light, which may perhaps also be read out of his remark (p. 137) on analogous experiments on *Leptynia*: ‘one takes pains to let them see the daylight only two or three times a week, the time to renew the broom leaves’. The pains may not have been very successful. His black *Dixippus* will still have received sufficient light reflected from dark surfaces. Dobkiewicz obtained black *Dixippus* from black and from red reflection boxes.

7. ‘Green 1’ occurred on 29 September 3 times in brilliant yellow, 6 times in white. Dobkiewicz obtained it singly in control cultures.

8. ‘Green 2’ was borne by all 8 transformed animals from grey, all 3 from darkness, further 3 from neutral tint, 2 from brilliant yellow, 1 each from medium violet, lemon yellow and bright red. In darkness MacBride and Jackson also always obtained green animals; it is not quite certain whether their green is to be identified with ‘green 1’ or ‘green 2’.

9. ‘Green 3’, which appears rarely at all, had not appeared on the first recording day (the second recording will report a single piece, and that in dark violet).

We gain a better overview if we do not trouble ourselves about single pieces and look to see which colour types occur in a given surrounding. It is shown there that mostly, in addition to the most frequently appearing colour type, there are also some pieces of the colour types standing nearest to it, connected with it directly by transitions and occasionally by the physiological colour change. Thus from neutral tint we find, besides ‘brown 2’, on the one hand ‘brown 1’, on the other the brownish ‘green 2’ represented; most clearly, with black surroundings, besides 6 black also 3 dark brown (‘brown 3’) are recognisable as the nearest-standing.

We find that *Dixippus* becomes light before light surfaces, dark before dark ones, brownish green (2) before medium ones and in the dark, blue, violet and red acting like dark surfaces. Contradictory for the present remains brilliant yellow, which indeed yields in part pure green (1) specimens, but also all kinds of dark ones, and white, which does not give the lightest pieces.

If we now pass, however, to the second recording, of 18 December, then all our findings, and with them also those of the earlier authors on the influence of coloured surroundings on the colouring of *Dixippus*, seem to have been overthrown. With few exceptions, which then mostly conform to what was ascertained earlier, the animals transformed later are, in all colours, of the type ‘green 2’!

The explanation for this can surely lie only in this, that with decreasing light intensity the illumination in December proved for all the colour boxes incapable of effecting a shift of the colour type from the ‘green 2’ valid for neutral illumination and darkness. If this assumption is correct, then a diminution of light intensity should also at another season of the year bring about such a shift, so that e.g. the action of blue light, which has a darkening effect, loses this property through reduction of the illumination strength. We meet here again the difference, repeatedly emphasised by us, between dark surfaces in good illumination and increasing darkness through decrease of the light. With this again are connected the questions whether the influences of variously coloured surfaces on the colour change depend merely on the intensity, or also on the quality, of the reflected light. The difference of the effect of darkness and of dark surfaces, which is so extraordinarily distinct in *Dixippus*, already proves, as in other similar cases, that the intensity of the light alone cannot be decisive. It still had to be tested whether particular properties of the reflection of light from walls (contrasts and so forth) or the quality of the light waves is decisive. For this purpose experiments with transmitted light were set up with some of the colours which had served for the reflection experiments, and indeed with various light intensities, of which the next section treats.

Here it may further be mentioned that the darkness animals recorded on 18 December showed, besides 2 ‘green 2’, no fewer than 8 ‘green 1’. The longer stay in darkness thus seems to have brought with it a further decrease of the dark pigment.

### 2. Experiments in transmitted light of various intensity.

In the blue series and the neutral tint, the difference between two cages standing side by side amounted to one layer of paper of the colour concerned. In the yellow series, to achieve a similar effect, one layer each of a paper of the same colour but thinner had to be added as well, since otherwise the least illuminated sixth cage still appeared to our eye far lighter than the sixth of the other colour series. From behind the cages were hung with a sheet of paper of the colour concerned, in order to prevent reflected light from penetrating from this side. All cages were exposed in front to the southern sun and were struck by it uniformly. Into each cage came, again in the summer of 1916, 15 young, of which at least 2/3 of each container reached the imaginal stage. If we further recall that de Sinéty behind a red glass plate had obtained specimens just as dark as we in the cage with red walls, then there can be no doubt that transmitted and reflected light of analogous intensity and wavelength exert the same influence on the colouring of *Dixippus*.

### 3. Experiments in darkness.

We had to anticipate the results of our experiments on the colouring of *Dixippus* which were kept in the dark, because they were needed as controls for the influence of reflected or transmitted light. Let therefore only a few words be added on the technique of the darkening and on occasional observations which were made on the cultures kept in darkness.

In order to avoid as far as possible temperature differences between the individual members of the experiments, all cages, both of the experiments in reflected and in transmitted light, were set up in one and the same room. Therefore it was refrained from setting up the darkness experiments in a separate darkroom, which through its double door closure would have prevented every penetration of traces of light. To be sure, it would then also have been necessary to undertake the change of food and other manipulations entirely in the dark. A special apparatus for accomplishing this without a darkroom has been described by MacBride and Jackson. Since, however, they obtained exactly the same results as we, namely the exclusive appearance of green specimens in darkness, our far simpler method is justified. This consisted in placing the cages used for the dark cultures under a light-tight bell cover, which at most could let slight traces of light shine through at the bottom. The change of food was undertaken as quickly as possible in the hours of weak daylight. The imagines showed themselves green without any exception. The darkness had apparently produced a certain retardation in their development, for on the 1st control day, on which otherwise half of all *Dixippus* were transformed, there were among 15 darkness animals only 3 (and these were ‘green 2’). Only on the 2nd control day were a further 10 *Dixippus* metamorphosed (among these were now 8 ‘green 1’). That this retardation was no accidental result emerges from the comparison of the 1st and 2nd recording at the various light intensities of the series in transmitted light. The smaller the quantity of light left, the fewer imagines does the first, the more the second control day show. Each of the three series shows this distinctly. The assumption of green colouring in the dark is thus paralleled by retardation of the transformation. It seems, then, that not merely the formation of the dark pigment, but the development of *Dixippus* as a whole suffers through exclusion of light. That secondary influences would be concerned here, such as too little intake of food in the dark, is little probable, since *Dixippus* is wont to feed only at night, and thus can hardly be disturbed in its food intake by lack of light. Retardation of development by keeping in the dark has also been observed elsewhere, e.g. by Hérouard on crustaceans, by Kammerer on fire salamanders.

Further deserving of attention in the dark cultures of *Dixippus* is the circumstance that the young hatching brown acquire a green colouring without the enjoyment of light, so that this green colour cannot be a matter of an animal pigment resembling plant chlorophyll and arising in the light. This would also be contradicted by its disappearance in the colour types which appear in various colours certainly not detrimental to chlorophyll. For this same reason it is also little probable that it could be a matter of plant chlorophyll itself, taken up with the food. Nor do the green pigment granules described by Schleip have any resemblance to chlorophyll granules. Chemically it is shown, on shaking alcoholic *Dixippus* extract with benzine, that indeed, as in plants, a more yellow constituent separates from a more green one, but, exactly the reverse of the green plants, the greener substance remains in the alcohol, while the yellower rises into the benzine (cf. Przibram–Brecher 1919, p. 139 and p. 178, serial nos. 387–397). Other reactions too show that we are dealing with lipochromes (as mentioned at the outset). Of *Leptynia attenuata*, closely related to *Dixippus*, de Sinéty (p. 137) likewise mentions that in the dark too it exchanges its at first yellow dress for a blue-green one, and that in the males, even with exclusion of light, a sap- to olive-green tone finally established itself just as in the light. Interestingly, de Sinéty remarks at this place that he observed no grey, that is pigmented, *Leptynia* in the darkness, while for *Dixippus* under the same experimental conditions he claimed to have obtained black specimens.

It is shown that in the II. generation there is indeed an increase of the colour-influenced specimens, but that in relation to the I. generation a lighter grade of a colour group is always favoured. Since already in the physiological colour change a colouring of *Dixippus* passes, with decrease of the light, into the next darker modification, the becoming-lighter could rest upon an on average higher light intensity which the II. generation enjoyed.

In fact, in consequence of the developmental cycle not lasting a whole year, the II. generation came to transformation in summer and therefore, owing to the season, enjoyed a greater fullness of light than the I. generation, transformed in autumn or even winter. It is thus a matter of the reverse side of the becoming-green in the dark winter months. Quite correspondingly, the exceptions can now be explained which present themselves in the examples h) and i), still to be discussed. In the darkness experiment h) an increase would have been possible in so far as, of the ‘green’ alone occurring, a greater percentage of ‘green 1’ could have appeared in the II. generation. That, however, is not the case; rather, ‘green 1’ are entirely absent, and beside the ‘green 2’ even brownish pieces appear singly: our exclusion of light has evidently now proved insufficient in the face of the stronger summer light. Still more striking is the grey experiment, example i), for, in complete contrast to the almost exclusively green I. generation, every green imago is absent in the II. Here the increasing light intensity must have become just great enough to enforce a brown pigmentation. There still remains to mention, as a second example of how little the colour type of the mother need be decisive for the offspring, our ‘red’ animal which appeared in control cages without special surroundings. Without change of the surrounding colour it has yielded not a single ‘red’ imago of the II. generation.

In general it may be said that through the action of the coloured surroundings on the II. generation as well, the number of colour-influenced imagines is raised from 1/2 to 3/4 of the *Dixippus* reared.

The small number of individuals in each single experiment is made good by the uniform outcome of all the experiments.

Individual cultures from transmitted blue light behaved quite similarly in this respect, and because of this defect they too shall not be discussed further. The example e), gold cinnabar, behaves similarly to the pooled cultures; in any case the maternal colour is not decisive. The dark culture r) does indeed show an increase of the mother-coloured offspring of a ‘green 2’, but since in the pooled culture a still greater percentage of ‘green 2’ appears in the II. generation, we are rather dealing with a direct action of the slight light intensity, which was nevertheless appreciable compared with the I. generation, and which in pooled as in individual culture let the brown specimens, entirely absent in the I. generation, come to life.

Summing up, we may say that the colour of the mother is not a matter of indifference for the offspring, even if both generations have transformed under the same light influences. Colour types which occurred in the I. generation in a low percentage can, from a single specimen of this colour, despite unchanged surroundings, yield up to 3/4 offspring of the same colour. Colour types which had furnished the main part in a particular surrounding in the mother generation, and then on average in pooled culture yield 3/4 offspring of the same colour, gave in individual culture up to 100%.

MacBride and Jackson were unable to establish any influence of the maternal colour in ordinary illumination. With them there were always only a few brown specimens among a far preponderant number of green ones, and this even when mother or grandmother were brown. The percentage they give for green specimens (over 90%) agrees entirely with that established by us for grey surroundings. In the grey pooled culture of the II. generation we likewise have no influence of the maternal colour; those transforming in the summertime were indeed all brown. Evidently, then, slight fluctuations of light brought about a reversal of the colouring, which just as easily switches back again independently of the maternal colour.

### 3. Increase of the colour influence in the II. generation despite counter-selection.

Since for the individual cultures specimens had to be taken from the pooled cultures, whose eggs henceforth no longer came among those of the pooled cultures, it might appear as if we had thereby committed a gross experimental error, because through a certain selection of the *Dixippus* to be isolated the colour of the offspring in the pooled culture was bound to give a different result than if these single animals had participated in the offspring of the pooled culture. It is the task of the present section to show that a falsification of the result by the removal of single females has mostly not only been avoided, but that the presence of the actually isolated specimens would in some cases even have had to yield a result still more favourable for the increase of the action than has been described above.

a) From the straw-yellow cage 1 ‘brown 1’ had been taken; nevertheless in the II. generation of the pooled culture the percentage of ‘brown 1’ had risen from 40 to 77. Since, however, the specimen taken showed no increase of ‘brown 1’ among its offspring, in this case a small decline would have had to be recorded had it been left in.

b) It would be similar with blue, had not the other (contradictory) outcome of the individual culture been attributable to the fading of the blue paper.

c) In gold cinnabar ‘brown 2’ had undergone in the individual culture a decline in favour of ‘brown 3’, but since in pooled as in individual culture both ‘brown’ together reach 100% in the II. generation, the presence of the isolated *Dixippus* would have been unable to change anything in this main result.

d) Least favourable for judgement are the conditions in the brilliant-yellow cage. All non-green *Dixippus* of the I. generation here made up 3/4 of the inmates; since besides 2 green ones 4 non-green were isolated, the ratio of the mothers in the pooled culture was shifted somewhat in favour of the brown ones, for these now made up somewhat more than 3/4. Since, however, of the individually reared ‘red’ and ‘black’ about half the offspring were green, their inclusion would likewise have had to turn out to the disadvantage of the non-green. It is therefore probable that the slight increases in the percentages of non-green specimens in the II. generation of the pooled culture are to be attributed to the exclusion of the 6 specimens, and that with an uncurtailed pooled culture no shift at all would have appeared in the II. generation. We shall, however, still have to return to the curious appearance of quite dark beside quite green colours in yellow light.

e) Mid-violet yielded ‘brown 3’ in the I. generation 43%, in the pooled culture of the II. generation ‘brown 3’ together with ‘brown 2’ 77%. The one isolated ‘brown 3’ yielded 100% offspring of the same colour, the other, ‘green 2’ isolated from mid-violet, 57% ‘brown 2’. Leaving the ‘brown 3’ in would therefore have raised the percentage of same-coloured above that of the pooled culture; leaving the ‘green 2’ in would by no means have lowered it approximately to the figure of the I. generation.

f) The ‘red 2’, which appeared without special surroundings and otherwise singly and yielded no offspring of the same colour, are of interest here only in so far as they demonstrate the futility of determining the colour of the offspring in *Dixippus* by selection of a particular colour type alone.

g) Black had yielded in the pooled culture of the I. generation 69% ‘black’ and ‘brown 3’, in the pooled culture of the II. generation 92% ‘brown 3’; the inclusion of the offspring of the isolated specimen, which yielded 100% of such dark brown ones, would improve the percentage still somewhat.

h) The darkness culture would, on adding in the offspring of the single isolated ‘green 2’, give a somewhat lower percentage of same-coloured offspring. Since, however, as mentioned, the appearance of non-green specimens in the dark has perhaps rested on access of light, no significance at all attaches to the slight difference.

The removal of a specimen of a particular colour type has, in examples a), b), e) and g), been unable to prevent, in the pooled culture of the II. generation, a rise in the percentage of the colour type characteristic of the surrounding concerned. In example c) (and h) a change of the percentage could not be expected, since in the pooled culture of the II. generation 100% same-coloured had already been reached. Only in d) may a trifling shift of the result have come about through the removal of several specimens.

In any case the results obtained in section III./1. on the reinforcement of the light influence in the II. generation cannot be traced back to the selection effected by the exclusion of single specimens. For such selections were either not undertaken at all or, in the isolation of single *Dixippus*, were undertaken precisely in such a way that less of the colour type characteristic of the particular surrounding colour remained in the pooled culture.

## IV. Unsuccessful Experiments of Various Kinds.

### 1. Influence of the male on the colouring of the offspring.

In our first *Dixippus* generation, originating from unfertilised females, 1 male ‘brown 1’ and 3 males ‘green 2’ appeared. The cultures concerned were not used as pooled cultures for the offspring. In the II. generation, likewise produced throughout parthenogenetically, one male imago ‘brown 1’ and two larvae of the same colour came under observation. We must therefore agree with MacBride and Jackson, against de Sinéty (pp. 128 and 240) and Meissner (p. 90), that males as well as females can arise from unfertilised eggs of *Dixippus morosus*. We should now gladly have undertaken pairings of animals of the same colouring, in order to obtain information on the mode of inheritance. De Sinéty (p. 138) had a female of the stick insect *Leptynia attenuata* with a flesh-coloured dorsal band covered by a brown male, and reared from this two brown males and a green female with the maternal dorsal stripe. A grey female, paired (perhaps first with a grey, then) with an olive-coloured male, yielded two green females. Green could thus be recessive with respect to dark-pigmented. This has been established in crossings of other Orthoptera, namely *Sphodromantis*, by one of us (Przibram 1909). Fryer has undertaken extensive cultures with yellow-brown and green stick insects of the genus *Clitumnus* (probably *cuniculus* Westw.) and likewise holds green to be recessive. He has apparently undertaken no experiments to influence the colour artificially. Mendelian ratios have also been found by Nabours in the hybridisation of variously coloured jumping grasshoppers of the genus *Paratettix*. However, neither a copulation of the *Dixippus* pairs placed in isolation cages, nor the laying of fertilised eggs, could be observed.

### 2. Influence of constant illumination during several generations.

It had originally been the plan to produce uniformly coloured cultures by sustained illumination of a particular kind, and only then to test the influence of transfer into other surroundings for one to several generations, as well as that of transfer back into the old or into intermediate conditions. But the present experiments have shown that, because of our change of seasons, a constant illumination is not to be achieved, while the change in the fullness of light seriously disturbs the exact execution of the colour-transmission experiments. A second deficiency rests also on the very fluctuating temperature to which the *Dixippus* transforming at different seasons are exposed, so long as we do not again have our temperature chambers at our disposal. For, according to earlier experiences on the related stick insects *Bacillus* and *Diapheromera*, a cooler temperature should favour green, a warmer one brown colouring, and in this way further enhance the difference between the colourings at different seasons. However favourable, therefore, *Dixippus* seemed to be for inheritance experiments in consequence of the easy parthenogenetic rearing and the possibility of three generations in one year, we have nevertheless decided to refrain from further use of this object until we shall be in a position to establish more exact conditions.

We were further strengthened in this by the tendency appearing in the yellow cultures to admit green and quite dark specimens side by side, for which the explanation shall be attempted in section V./2. In any case, however, this phenomenon is a hindrance to obtaining uniform cultures and thereby also easily interpretable rules of inheritance.

### 3. Influence of blinding on the colouring.

The enormous role which falls to the eye in many colour changes of animals made it appear desirable to eliminate a possible influence of the eyes by blinding. Imagines deprived of the eyes on both sides with an electrocautery needle could easily be kept alive for up to 14 days. No change whatever in the colouring occurred, whether green, brown or black specimens were used. Larvae of *Dixippus*, on the other hand, did not survive the same operation, so that unfortunately we have obtained no information on the role of the eye.

We have undertaken no experiments to eliminate vision by painting over the eyes of larvae with black paint, since this procedure would have to be renewed at least after every moult in order to give lasting results. Moreover, according to Stockard (pp. 52 and 58), the stick insects so blackened — he used imagines of *Aplopus Mayeri* — still perceive light and direct their movements according to the illumination, although reacting more slowly than the unvarnished ones. Schleip (p. 60), who repeated these experiments on *Dixippus* itself, could not find even such a constant difference in the reaction time.

## V. Explanations of the Positive Experimental Results.

### 1. Parallels between the colouring types of *Dixippus* imagines and *Pieris* pupae.

In earlier experiments with caterpillars of the cabbage white, *Pieris brassicae*, one of us (Brecher 1917) distinguished four main types of the colouring of their pupae, which corresponded to particular surrounding colours. Lightest pupae arose on light, white walls, medium ones on grey, blue-green and in darkness, dark ones on dark red, blue and black walls, finally green ones on yellow, yellow-red and yellow-green.

To the lightest pupae correspond *Dixippus* ‘brown 1’ in so far as here the otherwise green hypodermis pigment is entirely replaced by white-grey colour and dark pigment is almost entirely lacking. As we have seen, the *Dixippus* ‘brown 1’ occur on light walls (straw yellow, lilac, light green, white and light grey). On the other hand we find the darkest *Dixippus* types, black, red and brown 3, distinguished like the dark *Pieris* pupae by much dark brown pigment (melanin), most frequently on dark walls (mid- and dark violet, blue, red, black). Much less pronounced, however, is the assignment of the green colour to a particular surrounding. Histologically, ‘green 1’ of *Dixippus* would correspond to the green pupae through the vigorous appearance of the green pigment and the complete absence of the dark. But it appears not only in yellow, and there without prevailing, but also in darkness, where we should really have expected only medium ones, that is approximately our ‘green 2’. To be sure, in *Pieris* too the separation is not an entirely clean one, in that in the dark too pupae inclining more to green are no rarity. Besides *Dixippus* ‘green 2’ we must also regard ‘brown 2’ as medium types, which indeed appear almost everywhere, especially with indistinct colouring. The rare ‘red 1’ and ‘green 3’ are found again as rarer variants in the reddish and dark green pupae, without their production by a particular kind of illumination being as yet fully assured.

Since now, through our preliminary experiments on extracts and especially chemical investigations on *Pieris* pupae, it has been possible to point out the path of the colour influence by variously coloured light in the colour sensitivity of a ferment, the tyrosinase, it is natural to explain in the same way the analogous colour types of *Dixippus*, which seem to owe their origin to analogous surroundings. One can easily convince oneself that *Dixippus* too possesses active tyrosinase. We have described above in section I./3. how the different colour types are distinguished by a different efficacy of the tyrosinase. Since now the colour types appear with different frequency in different illumination, we have reason to assume a causal connection between light, efficacy of the tyrosinase and colour type. The lightest *Dixippus* arise, like the lightest *Pieris* pupae, on light walls, and such act on the one hand bleachingly upon lipochromes, on the other hand, chiefly through the ultra-red rays, retardingly upon the tyrosinase action; the tyrosinase of *Dixippus* ‘brown 1’ was found very weak (cf. Table C).

The darkest *Dixippus* arise, like the darkest pupae, on dark walls, in so far as these emit blue, violet or ultraviolet rays, which bring the action of the tyrosinase fully to development. The tyrosinase of the darkest *Dixippus* types, ‘black’, ‘red’ and, more slowly, ‘brown 3’, developed vigorous melanin formation in the tyrosine experiments (cf. Table C). Between these two colour groups the medium colour types of *Dixippus* range themselves in their tyrosinase action, corresponding to the medium *Pieris* pupae in the absence of a particular colour effect, namely ‘green 2’ and ‘brown 2’. Strikingly, however, the non-pigmented *Dixippus* type ‘green 1’ now showed a very vigorous tyrosinase, approaching the darkest or even surpassing these. While, therefore, yellow rays in *Pieris* pupae condition a deficit of melanin, which comes about through the yellow-irradiated tyrosinase becoming inactive, and always produce green pupal colour, we find in *Dixippus* both green and dark colour types linked with yellow surroundings, and the green colour type contains strongly active tyrosinase. The explanation of this different behaviour shall now be attempted in the following section.

The parallel between the behaviour of the pupal and the *Dixippus* colourings can be followed still further in the transmissibility of the modifications produced by the action of light. Just as in our experiments with the stick insects, upon the action of a particular surrounding colour on two generations in succession, the percentage of influenced imagines rose, so too in experiments with cabbage whites the percentage of typically coloured pupae has risen substantially after exposure of two generations, on which Dürken has reported in two preliminary communications. The publication of our own experiments on the transmissibility of the induced pupal colourings will, in consequence of the failure of the *Pieris* cultures in the first years of the experiments, take place only later.

### 2. Differences of colour formation between *Dixippus* and *Pieris*.

Since the chemistry and the histological position of the pigments in the stick insects and the pupae of the whites seem to be analogous, it is little probable that the differences which appear in the influence of the yellow colour would be attributable to a specific difference of their tyrosinases. This can be assumed all the less, since the tyrosinases of plant origin too, especially the one from honey fungus mostly used by us, exhibit a distinct colour sensitivity. Now, however, the whites possess the capacity for colour adaptation at only a single stage of development, namely the short time between the cessation of feeding at the end of the caterpillar period and the casting of the caterpillar skin. Only on the pupa do different colour types then come to light, which owe their origin to the action of different colours during the preceding sensitive stage. Quite otherwise in *Dixippus*: here various larval stages can be influenced and then show colour adaptation at the next moult. In our experiments we have taken only the imaginal moult into consideration: our colour types relate to the imagines, whereas those in *Pieris* relate to the pupal case.

In the pupae of butterflies, then, there is a quite definite stage present, upon the attainment of which definite colour adaptations can take place, whereas in the stick insects colour influence is possible at least from the second half of the larval period up to the imaginal moult (perhaps even beyond, cf. Meissner p. 19). Now indications have emerged that the attainment of the sensitive caterpillar stage is bound up with such a change of the reaction state of the caterpillar tissues that only thereby is the possibility given of colour influence such as *Pieris* pupae exhibit (Brecher, Sixth Part, 1921). An acidification seems to occur, which at first calls forth an acceleration of the tyrosinase action. Then the acidification recedes again, and indeed most slowly in yellow surroundings, most rapidly in black, which is effective through ultraviolet rays (Brecher 1919), and in white, whose effect rests on ultra-red rays (Brecher, Fifth Part, 1921). The tyrosinase which has become inactive through acidification experiences, through the alkalinising effect of non-yellow light, a reawakening which leads to the formation of dark pupae, but at too high a degree of alkalescence to the origin of light pupae. If we now wish to transfer these experiences to *Dixippus*, we can likewise expect that the yellow rays act acidifyingly, the blue to ultraviolet alkalinisingly.

The striking phenomenon that brilliant yellow yielded, besides green, also dark colour types of *Dixippus* seemed at first quite in contradiction with the characteristic influence of this colour on *Pieris* pupae, in which green pupae without black pigment always arise. Soon, however, the first short irradiation experiments with tyrosinase extracts from *Pieris* caterpillar blood (in September 1916, in Brecher VI, 1921) as well as from honey fungus (Przibram–Brecher 1919) revealed a certain agreement with the behaviour of the living *Dixippus*, while they behaved inversely to the colourings of the pupae, in that in yellow the strongest blackening of the ferment-containing samples occurred, in blue the least. The further continued irradiation experiments on ferment extracts both of *Pieris* (Brecher 1921) and of other animal and plant tyrosinases (Przibram and Brecher 1919, Przibram 1919, Przibram and Dembowski 1919) allowed us to become acquainted with the manner of the colour action on the colour-forming ferment, from which the behaviour of *Dixippus*, deviating with respect to the action of yellow, could also be explained without constraint. Indeed, according to this the behaviour of *Dixippus* in yellow even represents, so to speak, the realisation of a case theoretically postulated as possible (see Brecher VI, 1921, p. 83).

We have found from the above-mentioned investigations on the colour sensitivity of tyrosinase that the effect of the colours on the tyrosinase is not always the same. Shorter irradiation raises the efficacy of the tyrosinase from yellow, inhibits the efficacy of that from blue to ultraviolet. After longer irradiation this relation reverses. [Some results speak altogether for a periodicity of the colour effect, in that later yellow again conditions stronger efficacy than the other colours, etc.]

The tyrosinase of the *Pieris* caterpillars ripe for pupation shows after short irradiation the strongest efficacy in yellow and yellow-green, the weakest in blue to ultraviolet (experiments in September 1916, in Brecher VI, 1921), and upon a later removal from the colours an exhaustion of the tyrosinase in yellow and yellow-green and strong efficacy in red and black (ultraviolet rays) (Brecher VI, 1921). In the colouring of the pupae only this reversal of the colour effect comes to expression, in that surroundings reflecting ultraviolet yield the dark pupae, yellow on the other hand green pupae without black pigment.

We know that the action of the colours consists in a change of the reaction state which influences the efficacy of the tyrosinase. At pupation we are dealing, in consequence of physiological processes at metamorphosis, with a change of the reaction state exactly determined in time and degree. The action of the surrounding colours and the shifts in the reaction state called forth by them fall precisely into this period of reaction change in the caterpillars; the colour sensitivity is conditioned precisely by the latter — sensitive period. Now in the pupae both the time of the preparatory period and the course of the reaction, in a word the sensitivity, are so attuned to the mode of action of the colours that on the pupa the ‘reversal’ of the colour effect always comes about, which yields a colour adaptation. We have so far not succeeded, or at least not unobjectionably, in shortening the time of the action of the colours on the caterpillars — that is, in bringing them in at a later stage — in such a way that pupation should take place already during the strongest efficacy of the tyrosinase in yellow and weakest in ultraviolet, whereby dark pupae would then have to arise on yellow. Such experiments were begun on *Vanessa Io*.

It is otherwise with *Dixippus*, where we have before us no metamorphosis but a gradual transition to the imaginal stage. Presumably we may also have in *Dixippus* times of greater sensitivity to the surrounding colours (according to Vosseler, before the moult). We have not yet pursued this question in experiments of our own.

In any case it can hardly be restricted to the time before the last moult, since in one experiment, on bringing animals into the colours after the penultimate moult, we obtained no further influence. Certainly the colour influence is extended over a much greater period of time, since indeed in all other experiments the *Dixippus* stood in the colour conditions from hatching onwards.

We could therefore assume that the moult, and hence the moment for the pigment formation in the new skin, does not set in quite so uniformly for all specimens of an experiment, and that perhaps hereditarily fixed individual differences are decisive for this. If in yellow surroundings the moult sets in, in some specimens, precisely in the stage of the strongest efficacy of the tyrosinase, which would thus correspond, according to the processes in caterpillars and tyrosinase extracts, to the shorter duration of action of yellow, then it is evident that dark types must then arise. If the moulting process and the pigment formation set in at the moment of exhaustion of the tyrosinase, which corresponds to the longer action on the tyrosinase, then we have the same case as at pupation, and green *Dixippus* arise in yellow, as is always the case with the pupae.

Thus this occurrence side by side of green and dark *Dixippus* could be explained, and this apparent exception could confirm most beautifully the rule of the reversal of the colour effect.

That it could possibly be a matter here of a hereditarily fixed rhythm would be supported by the result with isolated types from brilliant yellow, which in the second generation distinctly show the influence of the maternal colour.

With pupation the process of colour formation is to a certain extent concluded; we speak of a ‘straight’ onset with shorter action and of a ‘reversal’, as it comes to expression in the pupal colour. In the test tube we find some indications that a periodicity of the colour effect is present. Thus, with irradiation of tyrosinase continued for a long time, the yellow pre-irradiated one is then again more effective than the others (Przibram and Brecher 1919). To be sure, it was only a persistence of the yellow pre-irradiated tyrosinase at the minimum reached earlier, while the tyrosinase from the other colour conditions has overtaken that from yellow in the increase of inactivity. A recovery of the yellow-irradiated tyrosinase up to the initial strength has never been noticed in vitro; but here, no doubt, the new production of tyrosinase is also mostly absent. Further, on the action of various degrees of caustic soda on the efficacy of the tyrosinase, a diversity of the efficacy showed itself which amounts to a wave line, with several optima and minima. On further removal and setting up of samples the points shift, the optima are at once exhausted and the minima rise towards a next optimum. A persistence of the first optimum has also been noticed, that is a kind of recovery (Brecher VI, 1921).

In the living animal, which stands continuously under the same colour influence, as in *Dixippus*, it is however easy to assume that in consequence of the life process the tyrosinase is replaced, and that upon a period of exhaustion in the same colour an effective recovery again follows. In favour of this speaks the strikingly effective tyrosinase from type green 1, and also the strong blackening of the first filtrate of this type in contrast to the weaker one of type black.

The undoubtedly present periodicity of the tyrosinase efficacy could, even if one thinks of it as continued into the next generation, furnish an explanation of the shift of the colour types from dark to light and from green to brown, such as neutral surroundings have yielded in the II. generation.

According to this, in the colouring of the parthenogenetic offspring of *Dixippus* reared in surroundings of the same colour, three factors would have to be taken into consideration:

1. The influence of the surrounding colour (is raised in the offspring, which is expressed in the percentage. Inheritance of induced characters).

2. The influence of the maternal colour on the offspring (inheritance of old characters).

In cases where the surrounding colour results in several colour types, as brilliant yellow, the isolated offspring of each colour type under the same colour yield predominantly animals of the colouring of the mother.

3. Periodicity of the tyrosinase efficacy, in that dark types tend to yield lighter-coloured offspring, and pure green types on the other hand yield offspring with somewhat more dark pigment. This occurs chiefly in neutral surroundings, where no particular colour effect conditions the tyrosinase efficacy.

### 3. *Dixippus* as an example of colour divergence in domesticated animals.

Schleip (p. 72) writes in his treatise on the colour change of *Dixippus*: ‘Another question, which is of interest in various respects, cannot yet be decided with our slight knowledge of the individuals of *Dixippus* living in their natural habitat. Do the colour varieties described also occur in nature? Brunner v. Wattenwyl and Redtenbacher (1908) state that the male is pale olive green, the female black. If in the wild really only black female individuals occur, then the supposition suggests itself that *Dixippus* has changed in captivity, and indeed under the effect of the parthenogenesis which occurs there as the sole mode of reproduction. The various lighter colourings appeared perhaps because the pigments causing the black appearance decreased more or less in quantity under the changed conditions.’

It is not quite evident how parthenogenesis is supposed to play a role in this. On the other hand, the possibility of the appearance of the various colours in consequence of captivity gains in probability through the experiments which have demonstrated an influencing of the colour in different illumination. In the Indian homeland, always exposed to the same illumination conditions, which are made particularly uniform by the almost equal length of all days in the tropics, occasions for colour change were not given. Since the females are considerably larger than the males, and hence probably pass through more moults, the sexual difference in colour could also rest upon the differently long influencing of the female by yellow-green light. Something similar would hold for *Aplopus Mayeri* of Tortugas, Florida, whose females according to Stockard (p. 46) vary only between grey and dark brown, whose males between mixtures of green and brown. In other species, however, precisely the males can be dark chocolate-brown, while the females are yellow-brown or green, thus in *Clitumnus cuniculus* (Fryer 1914); but here too the males are somewhat smaller. It is of course not to be denied that sexual differences in colouring may not also be secondary characters influenced by the gonad.

On the other hand, the species occurring in the European Mediterranean countries, *Bacillus rossii* and *B. gallicus*, *Leptynia attenuata* and *hispanica* (de Sinéty p. 130), already exhibit in the wild green, brown, red and other colours (and that in both sexes), such as *Dixippus morosus* likewise shows after its importation into Europe.

It is therefore not excluded that this species, just like its European relatives, has come to a divergence of the colourings through varying light conditions. With this an example would be gained of how the colouring of animals can experience a divergence through the change of external factors setting in with domestication.

## VI. Summary.

1. The permanent colour differences of transformed *Dixippus morosus* rest, not, like the physiological colour change of these stick insects, upon the migration of histological elements (pigment granules), but upon a different quantitative ratio of three pigments, a dark brown melanin, a green and an orange-red lipochrome.

2. The predominance of particular colour types is dependent on the illumination, and indeed the same colour of paper screens exerts the same influence whether reflected or transmitted light of the same intensity is used.

3. If the same illumination influence is brought to bear through two (parthenogenetic) generations, the percentage rises of specimens which bear the colour characteristic of the chosen influence.

4. Besides the influence of the external factor, however, the colour of the mother also makes itself felt in the colouring of her offspring, so that preceding modifications can thus be transmitted.

5. Similarly as in some butterfly pupae, e.g. *Pieris brassicae*, whitish surroundings produce light *Dixippus*, red, violet, blue and black surroundings dark ones, grey and darkness medium-coloured, namely greenish and brownish ones, and yellow pure green ones.

6. Besides the green, however, quite dark *Dixippus* also come to light in yellow surroundings, which is explained by the differently long action of yellow rays, whereas in the butterfly caterpillars only the same short sensitive stage is ever open to the colour influence. In contrast to the pupal experiments hitherto, pure white also produces green *Dixippus*.

### Postscript.

In the Umschau XXV, 492, 1921, A. Schmitt-Auracher has published a preliminary communication on colour change in *Dixippus*. It appears to have carried out with positive success the experiment which failed with us, of preventing the colour change by elimination of the eyes. The colours communicated of the stick insects when kept in variously coloured surroundings agree with ours and with those of the earlier authors. On the other hand we are unable to concede the justification of its conclusions, tailored to Hess’s thesis of the total colour-blindness of all insects. Let it for the present merely be recalled that the *Dixippus* were green in darkness, with the use of grey surroundings and with white, which excludes that the blackening effect of the blue and violet rays rests merely on the perception of different intensities.
